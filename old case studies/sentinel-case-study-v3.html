TL;DR — For the scanners

The bottleneck in wildfire response is cognitive, not technological. Sentinel redesigns the interface as a decision surface — the AI handles triage and execution, the human owns the three moments where judgment is irreplaceable.

Every single design decision in this project is an answer to the same question: what does the operator need to do right now — and what can the system safely carry for them?

01 — The Problem

48 minutes. And it's not a drone problem.

California already has the hardware. It doesn't have an interface worthy of it.

Sensor networks detect thermal anomalies within seconds. Drones can be airborne in under a minute. And yet the average detection-to-deployment window for wildfire response sits at 48 minutes. Every solution in this space tries to fix the hardware. Faster drones. More sensors. Wider satellite coverage.

None of it moves the number. Because the number isn't a hardware problem.

What actually happens in those 48 minutes

A thermal alert fires at 3:47 AM. The operator receives it — alongside three other alerts from different zones, a satellite feed on a second monitor, and a ground sensor log that hasn't been checked since the last shift handoff. They start mentally assembling a threat picture. Is this a controlled burn? What's the wind doing? How close is the nearest residential cluster? Who's on-call at Station 12?

None of those questions require human judgment. Every single one can be answered by a system with access to the same data. But the system doesn't assemble the picture — it just delivers the raw feeds. So the operator does the assembly, under time pressure, at 4 AM, after 6 hours of shift. That assembly process is where the 48 minutes go.

The actual problem statement: No interface currently exists that handles the cognitive assembly work between detection and deployment decision. Operators are doing machine-speed data aggregation with human-speed brains, under conditions specifically engineered to produce errors.

What Sentinel proposes

A strict division of labor. The system does the assembly. The operator makes the judgment. The system executes the decision.

Sensor fusion + cross-referencing

Drone routing + dispatch
Terrain + fire spread modeling

The AI system is named TALON. Every autonomous action it takes is logged under the TALON callsign with a timestamp, separated from operator actions in the audit trail. This isn't branding — it's accountability. TALON can be audited. "AI" cannot.

What we explicitly didn't build — No manual drone joystick controls. No mobile app. Operators authorize outcomes, not actions. Manual flight controls invert the entire human-AI relationship and recreate the exact micromanagement bottleneck this system exists to eliminate. A junior designer shows everything they built. A senior designer explains what they threw away.
02 — The Messy Middle

My first prototype tried to automate the crisis. It accidentally removed the human from the loop.

The earliest wireframes came from a single research finding: operators under extreme stress experience cognitive tunneling — they fixate on one data point and lose peripheral awareness. The obvious design response felt elegant: compress the decision tree. Remove the complexity. Make it impossible to get lost.

The Escalation Slider — what it was

A horizontal control at the bottom of the screen. The operator drags a handle from Assess → Contain → Rescue. Each position triggers a predefined AI macro-script: a set of drone deployments, evacuation calculations, and notification sequences that execute automatically when the slider reaches that stop.

Early wireframe · V0.1
Escalation Slider concept
[ Wireframe placeholder — insert early prototype image ]

It looked slick. Three clean states. One draggable handle. Felt like decisive, confident UX. It was psychologically dangerous.

Why it was wrong

A slider implies a linear, rigid script. If Asha drags to Contain, she is blindly trusting a black-box execution without seeing the geometry of the containment zone, the specific drone positions proposed, or which residential structures fall inside the projected spread ring. She's not making a decision. She's triggering a macro.

A slider doesn't record a conscious decision. It records a gesture. And when 47 households are evacuated based on that gesture, who is accountable? The audit trail says "operator moved slider to Contain at 03:48:22." That is not authorization. That is abdication.

Furthermore — the wireframes themselves were still cluttered. I had built a dark-mode dashboard with drone telemetry panels, altitude readouts, and sensor graphs covering roughly 120 text elements on screen. I had designed something that looked like it was solving cognitive load while actually adding to it.

04 — Visual Architecture

The map is not the UI. That distinction is everything.

Designing a command center map has two failure modes. The first: a flat, lifeless interface that looks like a Google Maps screengrab — no terrain depth, no sense of physical threat, no understanding that wind and gradient determine where fire goes. The second: 3D drones and 3D fire trucks on a 3D mountain — which looks like a mobile strategy game and communicates nothing about the lethality of the environment.

Sentinel escapes both by establishing a strict architectural separation between two layers that serve completely different purposes.

Reality Layer
[ Insert: 3D terrain base — photorealistic foothills ]
The Map is Reality — 3D Photorealistic topography, fire volume, wind-constrained terrain. Communicates the physical threat: gradient, vegetation density, how the fire will move. This layer cannot be clean. It is the unpredictable physical environment.
Intelligence Layer
[ Insert: 2D vector overlay — zones, drones, routes ]
The UI is Intelligence — 2D Drone positions, zone boundaries, spread estimates: infinitely scalable Figma vectors floating above the reality layer. This layer must be clean, crisp, and instantly legible. It is the structured system operating over the unpredictable environment.
Visual Silence: The separation creates a perceptual hierarchy where the operator instantly distinguishes between what is happening in the physical world (the 3D base) and what the system is doing about it (the 2D overlay). Neither layer competes for the same visual depth plane. This is what makes the interface readable at 4 AM under cognitive load.

This architectural decision also directly enables the smoke degradation edge case: because the intelligence layer is fully independent of the camera feed, TALON can fall back to LiDAR terrain reconstruction when smoke obscures optical feeds — and the operator loses video without losing spatial awareness. The map keeps working. The mission continues.

05 — Design Decisions

Six pivots. Each one moved the authority boundary.

These aren't visual refinements. Each pivot represents a change in where the line sits between what TALON owns and what Asha owns. Expand any card for the full reasoning and visual reference.

1
Forced navigation → interrupt design
Alert snapping to a new screen destroyed spatial context. Redesigned as a modal overlay that preserves the operator's current map state.
Visual reference · Pivot 01
[ Insert: side-by-side — old full screen switch vs. new modal overlay on map ]
Before
Anomaly detected → entire UI jumps to an Alert screen. Operator's spatial context wiped on every alert.
After
Alert arrives as floating modal over the live map. Spatial context intact. Operator responds without disorientation.

Operators don't have "alert time" and "non-alert time" — they're always mid-task. This pivot also crystallised a fundamental distinction: an interrupt demands a decision on top of current state. A transition means the operator commits to an entirely new phase. Once that was clear, every subsequent navigation decision in the system became easier to make.

2
Replay verification → live signal classification
A "verification replay" clip felt reassuring but was factually impossible. Replaced with TALON's ignition probability reasoning shown transparently.
Visual reference · Pivot 02
[ Insert: alert modal showing classification inputs — prior burns, wind vector, thermal delta ]
Before
A short "verification replay" — show the operator a clip to build confidence. Borrowed from consumer product thinking.
After
TALON classification: ignition probability with contributing factors surfaced — prior burn history, wind vector, thermal delta above baseline.

Fire doesn't have a replay. Detection comes from thermal sensors and satellites — there is no video record of an anomaly appearing. The deeper failure: we were designing trust through familiarity rather than accuracy. A score alone gives Asha nothing to push back against. Showing the reasoning gives her the basis to agree — or to say TALON is wrong.

3
Uniform density → progressive information scaling
~120 text elements across every phase caused cognitive paralysis during active rescue. Information density now scales inversely with operational urgency.
Visual reference · Pivot 03
[ Insert: 4 panel states side-by-side — Scan (~120 elements) through Rescue (~25 elements) showing density progression ]
Before
Same ~120 text elements across all phases. Paragraphs of AI rationale visible during active evacuation.
After
Scan: verbose context. Contain: headlines + status dots. Rescue: zero prose, ~25 elements, large numbers, one log entry.

Under stress, humans process numbers and spatial cues measurably faster than prose. The interface must breathe with Asha's cognitive load. Every panel component needed three render states. The Intel panel in Scan is a different component from the Intel panel in Rescue — same position on screen, different contract with the operator.

4
Action toolbox → consequence-tiered action bar
"Notify Authorities" sitting next to "Emergency Evacuate" in the same row. Proximity creates accidental authority.
Visual reference · Pivot 04
[ Insert: before/after action bar — flat row of all actions vs. tiered primary/secondary with chevron tray ]
Before
All actions in one scrolling row. Administrative tasks shared visual weight with life-safety decisions.
After
Saving Lives > Staging Hardware > Filing Reports. Primary actions visible. Secondary behind chevron tray. In Rescue: no primary CTA — its absence is the signal.

In a crisis, the cognitive cost of avoiding a destructive action you can see is always present. The action bar communicates system state through what's on it and what isn't. The empty Rescue bar is not an oversight — it signals correct execution without Asha needing to check anything.

5
Free-text evacuation form → pre-staged team chips
Operator typing "Route A, 15 personnel" during peak stress. Replaced with role-based team assignment staged a phase earlier.
Visual reference · Pivot 05
[ Insert: old free-text modal vs. new team chip assignment with zone selection ]
Before
Evacuation modal with free-text HTML inputs. Operator types route, types headcount. "15 personnel" is not how ground teams work.
After
Role-based team chips (Alpha Lead, Bravo, Charlie). Assign team to zone. System filters valid zones by certification. Pre-staged in Contain — not decided under Rescue pressure for the first time.

Typing during a stress spike forces translation from spatial reality to words. But the more important change is pre-staging: by the time Asha reaches Rescue, team assignments are already authorized. The confirmation modal becomes a review of a staged plan — not a first-time decision at maximum pressure.

6
Anonymous AI → TALON, auditable system agent
"AI Confidence 84%" is a black box. TALON logs every action under its callsign. Two different things.
Visual reference · Pivot 06
[ Insert: activity log comparison — "AI: action" vs. "TALON · 03:47:18 UTC: corroboration threshold crossed" ]
Before
"AI Confidence," "AI Map," "AI Recommends." Generic labels. No attribution. No audit trail.

TALON callsign. Every AI action logs separately from operator actions in cyan. Aerospace nomenclature throughout: Anomaly Validation, Telemetry Log, TALON Spatial Reconstruction.

This isn't branding — it's an accountability mechanism. When Asha is managing 47 households at 4 AM, she needs to know which decisions were hers and which were the system's. TALON can be audited. "AI" cannot. If it sounds like a consumer chatbot, she won't trust it with human lives. It shouldn't.

06 — Edge Cases

A wildfire at 4 AM is not a controlled environment.

Thick smoke severs drone feeds. The AI classification model gets it wrong. Dozens of minor faults surface simultaneously. Each of these is a designed state — not an unhandled error. We designed for the happy path last.

📡
Edge Case 01 · Signal
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
The reality
Dense smoke cover and mountainous terrain sever visual camera feeds in active wildfire zones. This happens in exactly the moments the operator most needs live footage. In most systems, this produces a "Connection Lost" modal and a gap in the map.
TALON fallback
When a drone loses optical feed, the UI does not throw a blocking error. TALON automatically falls back to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction. The operator loses live video — but retains spatial awareness, drone position, and telemetry. The mission continues. The degraded state surfaces as a single status chip in the left panel. Not a modal. Not an interruption. A status indicator that Asha can acknowledge when cognitive load permits. This fallback is also why the map's 3D base layer exists: when the camera can't see through smoke, the terrain model must carry the spatial picture alone.
⚡
Edge Case 02 · AI Error
AI classification wrong — the override flow
The reality
TALON's thermal corroboration model will sometimes be wrong. Or Asha will spot a geographic variable the model missed — a dry creek bed that's a fire corridor, a road that's blocked, a structure the satellite imagery hasn't updated in 6 months.
Override flow
TALON is the First Officer. Asha is the Captain. If the proposed containment perimeter is wrong, she taps Override. The UI shifts from Authorization Mode back to Manual Staging Mode — full geographic control transfers back to the human. Drone deployments already underway are not aborted; the plan modifies around what's in motion. Every override logs under Asha's operator ID, not TALON's. This is the hardest interaction in the system to design — and the least tested. If I had another iteration, real field dispatchers would stress-test this flow specifically. It's where Sentinel is most likely to break.
🔔
Edge Case 03 · Alert Fatigue
Exception management — preventing the noise that kills signal
The reality
With 6 drones airborne in a fire zone, there will be dozens of minor faults per hour: battery dips, slight route deviations, temporary relay signal losses. If every fault throws a red modal, Asha will stop reading them — and miss the one that matters.
Exception feed
Non-critical failures route silently to the Exception Feed in the right panel. Never to a blocking modal. Asha can batch-acknowledge them when cognitive load permits. Only failures that require a judgment call — a decision the system genuinely cannot make — interrupt the primary visual space. The design problem isn't building a good alert. It's building a good filter.
Last resort: ABORT MISSION
Every scenario above has a designed response. But for failures that exceed system scope — cascading drone hardware failure, total comms blackout, a scenario TALON has no model for — ABORT MISSION is a persistent secondary action in the action bar. Always one tap. Always the same position. No confirmation modal required. When something is catastrophically wrong, Asha should not be hunting through menus to stop it.
07 — Reflection

The honest version.

The 90-second claim is a hypothesis, not a proof.

The actual binding constraint in wildfire response may not be the interface at all. It may be radio protocol between dispatch and field units, or network latency in smoke-heavy terrain where drone feeds degrade, or the fact that TALON's classification model was trained on historical data that doesn't account for the specific burn patterns of a changing climate. I designed for the interface variable without validating whether it's the bottleneck.

What I'd do differently

The override flow is the hardest interaction in this system and the part I'd spend the most time on in a next iteration. It's the moment where the human-AI authority boundary is most contested — where Asha knows something TALON doesn't, and the system has to transfer control gracefully without losing what's already in motion. I designed the happy path for that flow. I didn't stress-test it with real dispatchers. That's the gap.

What I'd measure if this shipped: Time from alert interrupt to operator decision (dispatch / dismiss). Decision accuracy — dismissed alerts that became incidents. Operator override rate — too low may mean Asha trusts TALON more than she should. Exception acknowledgment delay — how long non-critical faults sit unacknowledged before they compound into something critical.

What comes after

Sentinel was designed for single-incident command. The next version needs to handle multi-incident coordination — simultaneous fires across multiple sectors, parallel command contexts, operator handoffs between shift changes. The system architecture supports it; the interface doesn't yet. Designing for state-of-emergency conditions, where it's not one fire but several simultaneous crises — fire, flood, infrastructure failure — is the obvious direction. Basically: the movies. Zombie apocalypse-level coordination. That's the next design problem.

"I started designing screens. I ended up designing a decision-making system under time pressure. The two things are not the same — and every pivot in this case study is the specific moment I noticed the difference."

The interface disappears during action. What remains is exactly the decision the operator needs to make — nothing more.
