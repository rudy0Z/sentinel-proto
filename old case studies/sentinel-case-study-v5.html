§01 · The problem

Operators were the bottleneck — not the hardware

When a sensor detects a potential fire, deploying drones over populated areas requires verification first. That means checking thermal readings, cross-referencing satellite data, and confirming burn permits across three separate systems — all manually, under time pressure. By the time an operator had assembled enough context to act, an average of 48 minutes had passed.

48 min
Average time from initial alert to first authorized action. Spent manually assembling context across disconnected systems.
90 sec
Target time to verify the incident and send an authority notification. Staging and containment follow after.
3
Explicit operator decisions in the entire verification window. Everything else is handled by the AI — with a full audit trail.
Root cause

The three systems operators relied on — thermal sensors, satellite feeds, and municipal burn permits — had no shared interface. Operators were manually translating between them under pressure. That translation work, not hardware capability, was the 48 minutes.

Validation note

The 90-second target is based on simulation walkthroughs against documented emergency dispatch protocols — not field-measured data from real operators. This gap is addressed honestly in the outcomes section.

§02 · System structure

Two operating modes, separated by a single checkpoint

The interface runs in two modes. The first 90 seconds focus entirely on verifying whether the incident is real and notifying civil authorities. Everything after — drone staging, evacuation coordination, ground team management — happens in the second mode once that notification is sent. The interface automatically adjusts what it shows based on which mode is active.

Phase A · Intelligence · 0–90 sec
Is this a real fire? Should authorities be notified?

Operator verifies the anomaly, reviews evidence, resolves any data conflicts, and sends the authority notification. Interface shows everything relevant — full sensor context, AI reasoning, and evidence sources.

Phase B · Operations · Post-notification
Coordinate resources, evacuate residents, supervise containment.

Drones executing, evacuation active, biometrics live. Interface automatically strips to compact single-line telemetry — no prose, exceptions only. The operator supervises rather than directs.

§03 · The verification flow

From thermal alert to authorized notification in 90 seconds

The operator — a sector lead named Asha managing multiple autonomous drone loops — receives four phase transitions during the verification window. TALON works silently between them. Each transition fires only when there's a real decision to make.

Scan
0 – 15 sec
An alert fires. TALON has already assembled a brief from available sensor data. Asha reads it and makes one decision: dispatch a verification drone or dismiss.
What the system does

Sensor Fusion assembles the incident brief before the alert fires — thermal readings, prior incident history, current burn permits. Asha gets a complete picture rather than raw data inputs.

What Asha does

Reviews the brief without leaving the map view. The primary action is Dispatch Scout-02. No context switching, no system hopping.

Anomaly alert — evidence brief overlay
assets/sentinel/phase-scan.png
Verify
15 – 45 sec
Scout en route. TALON fuses incoming sensor data in the background. The interface stays quiet — no updates unless something genuinely changes.
What the system does

Fire Behavior and Values at Risk agents run silently. TALON only fires the next interrupt when evidence crosses a meaningful threshold — three independent sensor confirmations minimum. If sensor and permit data conflict, the Forced Calibrator gate fires (see FS-01).

What Asha does

Continues monitoring other zones. She's not watching a progress bar — TALON will interrupt when there's something to decide. If there's no conflict, the next interrupt arrives with fully converged evidence.

Scout en route — agent pipeline active in right panel
assets/sentinel/phase-verify.png
Escalate
45 – 90 sec
Evidence has converged. The notification packet is ready. Asha reviews each source individually and authorizes the notification to civil authorities.
What the system does

Displays an evidence matrix — each contributing sensor as a labeled chip with its own confirmation status, not a single confidence score. Structures at risk shown prominently. The notification packet is assembled and waiting, but nothing happens until Asha approves it.

What Asha does

Reviews the individual evidence sources — she can see exactly which data is confirmed and which is pending. Clicks "Notify Authorities." The 90-second timer stops. The interface shifts to Phase B.

Operations
Post-notification
Notification sent. TALON executes. The interface strips to essentials — Asha monitors and intervenes when something needs a human call.
What the system does

Left panel converts from detailed drone cards to single-line telemetry strips. Ground crew biometric feeds go live. TALON handles execution autonomously — surface-only exceptions that require a decision.

What Asha does

Monitors the exception feed. Drag-and-drop asset repositioning available on the map if she needs to override a routing decision. If no action buttons are visible, the system is executing as planned.

§04 · Design decisions

Five decisions with meaningful trade-offs

These were the choices that had the most impact on how the system behaves — each one required giving something up to get the more important thing right.

D-01
Limiting the 90 seconds to verification, not full containment
The original prototype claimed full wildfire containment could be authorized in 90 seconds — including drone staging, evacuation, and residential clearance. That's not operationally possible before an incident is even verified.
Core scope
A
Keep the full-containment claim
Dropped — real emergency protocols require verification before any tactical deployment. The claim fails as soon as someone with field experience looks at it.
B
Remove the human gate — let the AI authorize responses automatically
Dropped — autonomous drone deployment over residential areas requires explicit operator authorization. This is a legal and regulatory constraint, not a design preference.
C
90 seconds ends at authority notification — containment follows in Phase B
The notification packet is sent with Asha's explicit sign-off. Staging and containment proceed after, without an artificial time constraint.
Trade-off
The original metric sounds more impressive. The chosen framing is what actually holds up in a real emergency dispatch conversation.
Outcome: The 90-second timer stops when Asha clicks "Notify Authorities." No artificial pressure on the coordination work that follows.
D-02
Requiring explicit confirmation when sensor data and permit records conflict
Under time pressure, operators tend to accept AI recommendations without scrutinizing them — a known pattern in emergency dispatch called automation bias. In wildfire response, this leads to false-positive deployments over residential areas.
AI governance
A
Let TALON silently resolve permit conflicts in the background
Dropped — sensor and satellite data don't always update at the same rate. A silent resolution can commit to the wrong conclusion before the conflict has actually resolved.
B
Block the interface with a modal until satellite data catches up
Dropped — covering the map canvas during an active size-up removes the spatial context Asha needs. A modal here is solving the wrong problem.
C
Surface the conflict as a warning, lock the primary actions, require a manual verification checkbox
Called the Forced Calibrator. Asha must confirm she's checked the permit database before any action is re-enabled. The map stays fully visible throughout.
Trade-off
Adds 4–6 seconds of mandatory friction on every permit conflict. That friction is proportional to what it prevents: a drone deployment over occupied housing based on incomplete information.
Outcome: The Forced Calibrator is the only deliberate speed penalty in the system. Every other decision is optimized for reducing latency — this one intentionally adds it.
D-03
Showing individual sensor readings rather than a single confidence percentage
TALON originally surfaced a single number: "87% ignition probability." Operators could accept or reject the recommendation, but had nothing concrete to scrutinize or push back on.
AI transparency
A
Single confidence score
Dropped — a number that can't be interrogated reduces the operator to someone who can only accept or reject. It doesn't support a meaningful override.
B
Confidence score with a paragraph explanation
Dropped — reading a prose explanation doesn't fit a 90-second decision window. The format doesn't support rapid scanning.
C
Evidence matrix — one chip per sensor source, each showing its own confirmation state
Thermal delta, LiDAR reading, satellite IR, burn permit status — each labeled separately so Asha can see exactly which input is confirmed, pending, or conflicting.
Trade-off
Takes more screen space than a single number. Worth it because operators who understand which specific signal drove a recommendation make better overrides — and correct overrides improve the system over time.
Outcome: When TALON is wrong, Asha knows which sensor to scrutinize. She's not just rejecting a black box.
D-04
Switching to vector coordinates when cell bandwidth becomes unusable
Remote wildfire perimeters run on degraded cell connectivity, not fiber. At latencies above 300ms, HD video feeds saturate the available bandwidth. Standard interfaces freeze behind loading indicators — which in an active incident is operationally useless.
Infrastructure
A
Show loading indicators and wait for the feed to buffer
Dropped — a frozen interface during an active response is worse than a degraded one. Spatial awareness gaps during rescue operations carry direct operational cost.
B
Fall back to audio-only radio coordination
Dropped — eliminates all spatial awareness of drone positions, evacuation routes, and zone boundaries. Not a viable fallback for a command interface.
C
Drop HD video, fade satellite imagery, and prioritize lightweight LiDAR vector coordinates
When latency exceeds 300ms, the interface automatically switches modes — satellite opacity drops to near-zero, drone positions and paths remain fully live as vector overlays.
Trade-off
Asha loses the camera feeds. A responsive interface with accurate coordinates is more useful than a high-fidelity one arriving 30 seconds late.
Outcome: Under simulated 340ms degradation, full spatial awareness was maintained with zero interface lockup. The system is designed for real-world connectivity, not ideal lab conditions.
D-05
Automatically reducing interface density as the incident escalates
The same amount of information was displayed across all phases — including during peak Operations, when the operator's attention is most divided across active coordination tasks.
Cognitive load
A
Separate layout per phase
Dropped — forces Asha to reorient her spatial model at a moment when cognitive overhead is already high.
B
Manual density toggle — let the operator choose what they see
Dropped — adds a preference decision at the worst possible moment. Operators shouldn't be configuring their interface during an active incident.
C
Automatic density scaling — same layout grid, less information shown as urgency increases
Detailed drone cards in the Intelligence phase. Compact single-line strips in Operations. The spatial model stays consistent — only the depth of information changes.
Trade-off
The operator has less control over their information view. The trade-off is justified because reducing available decisions during peak load is a feature, not a limitation.
Outcome: The interface simplifies as the incident escalates. Asha's spatial model never resets — only the density of what's shown within that model changes.

§06 · What I changed

Two early directions that didn't work

V0.1 — discarded
A slider for moving between response stages
V0.1 — slider escalation model
assets/sentinel/iter-v01-slider.png
Why it didn't work

Moving a slider felt like adjusting a setting, not authorizing a deployment. During testing, operators described it as a volume knob — they were moving the UI forward, not making a conscious decision. The gesture eliminated the moment of approval entirely.

V1 scope — corrected
Claiming full containment could be authorized in 90 seconds
V1 framing — original 90-second scope
assets/sentinel/iter-v01-90claim.png
Why it didn't work

Staging containment lines requires knowing the fire's size and direction. Evacuating residents requires knowing which areas are at risk. Neither is possible before verification. Correcting this changed the scope of the entire design — the 90 seconds became a verification-only window, not a lifecycle claim.

§08 · Results and reflection

What the design achieved and what I'd do differently

Primary validation
Time to first decision

From alert to dispatch action. Target under 15 seconds. If consistently above that, the brief format isn't surfacing the right information first — a design problem, not a training problem.

Safety signal
Dismissed alert accuracy

What percentage of dismissed alerts turned out to be real incidents? Anything above 2% requires immediate design review. The goal is accurate dismissals, not zero dismissals.

AI trust calibration
Operator override rate

Target range: 15–40%. Too low means operators are rubber-stamping recommendations. Too high means TALON's plans aren't reliable enough to act on. Both are failure modes.

Governance check
Calibrator false-positive rate

How often does the Forced Calibrator fire on a genuine fire (not a controlled burn)? Friction should be proportional to actual conflict probability — not a fixed tax on every alert.

What still needs real validation
The 90-second target comes from simulation walkthroughs, not from field-testing with trained emergency operators. Whether the Forced Calibrator's friction is acceptable under actual dispatch pressure is something I couldn't determine without real users. TALON's agent latency under genuine network degradation is also untested.
What I'd change if I started again
I'd spend less time on the Operations density transitions and more time testing the evidence matrix format with actual operators. The key question I never answered: does the chip-based layout match how dispatchers actually reason about incident verification, or does their mental model work differently? I assumed it would — I don't have data to confirm it.
If the system can make a call without Asha knowing,
Asha isn't in control — she just thinks she is.
