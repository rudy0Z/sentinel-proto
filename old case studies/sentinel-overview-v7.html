What it is
A desktop command center for wildfire response operators managing autonomous drone fleets. The AI handles sensor fusion, terrain modeling, and drone routing. The operator makes three explicit decisions.

Core idea
Each time the AI interrupts the operator, the work for that decision is already done. The operator is never handed raw feeds. They're handed a finished brief.

The Problem and What Sentinel Does

The drones were ready in 30 seconds. Operators were still taking 48 minutes to respond.

FlytBase makes autonomous drone infrastructure for public safety. Their drones can launch without a pilot, stream live thermal footage, and coordinate across a fleet — all without human input. In a wildfire scenario, a drone can be airborne and feeding data within 30 seconds of detection. The hardware was never the bottleneck. The interface sitting between that hardware and the human operator was.

The AI shows its reasoning, not just a classification
Instead of a confidence score, the operator sees the inputs behind it: prior burn history, wind vector, thermal delta. They have something to evaluate and push back on, not just a number to accept or reject.
The interface simplifies as the emergency peaks
Scan phase: full context. Rescue phase: drone status strips and structure clearance counts only. The layout stays constant. What changes is how much information is on screen — and that change is deliberate.
Every action is traceable to a person
TALON's actions log in cyan. The operator's log in white. Always visually separated, timestamped, permanently auditable. No ambiguity about whether a human or the system made any call.
Designed for failure, not just the clean path
Signal degradation, voice plan corrections, operator overrides, autonomous battery handoffs. All four failure states surface the problem as information rather than an alarm with no path forward.

The 90-Second Flow

Four phases. Each designed around what the operator needs to process at that moment.

The interface layout stays constant across all four phases. What changes is information density — not the operator's spatial model.

Scan
0 – 15 sec
Alert arrives as a pre-built brief. Operator reads the situation and makes Decision 1.
What the system does
Automatically fuses sensor data, satellite imagery, and local burn history into a ready-to-read brief. This replaces the 11 minutes operators previously spent cross-referencing three separate systems themselves.
What it does for the operator
Asha reads the brief without leaving the map. No context switch, no spatial reorientation. She makes her first decision, dispatch a drone or alert a ground team, in seconds rather than minutes.
Verify
15 – 45 sec
Drone en route. System works silently and fires a second interrupt only when evidence is ready.
What the system does
Alert folds to a compact en-route card in the sidebar. The drone is visible on the map. TALON works silently. It will not interrupt Asha again until the thermal evidence crosses a meaningful threshold.
What it does for the operator
Asha returns to monitoring other zones. She is not staring at a loading indicator. The second decision only fires when there is something worth deciding — not before.
Contain
45 – 75 sec
System delivers a complete containment plan. Operator reviews and physically commits to authorize.
What the system does
Delivers a complete plan before asking for authorization: drone positions, team assignments, zone priorities, evacuation routes. One number shown large: 47 structures in projected path. All proposals labeled PENDING AUTHORIZATION.
What it does for the operator
Asha reviews the full plan, then commits: press and hold AUTHORIZE for 2 seconds. The hold replaces a tap-through confirmation. A tap is proportional to sending a message. A 2-second hold is proportional to authorizing drone deployment over 47 occupied homes.
Rescue
75 – 90 sec
System executes. Operator supervises. The absent action button is the signal.
What the system does
Execution is fully autonomous: drones rerouting, teams coordinating, evacuation guidance active, structure clearance ticking. The interface shifts from briefing to reporting. The system has been handed everything it needs to operate.
What it does for the operator
The absent action button tells Asha the system is executing correctly. She monitors an exception feed and intervenes only when TALON surfaces something it cannot resolve. Everything else is handled.
Key Design Decisions

Three decisions that changed how the operator and the system share responsibility.

01
Dragging a slider to escalate was replaced with reading a plan and explicitly approving it
One slider control: drag from Assess to Contain to Rescue. Each position fires a pre-set script. Drones deploy to positions the operator never reviewed. → The system proposes a specific plan. The operator reads it and explicitly approves every single time, no exceptions.
The slider seemed like a speed improvement. But a gesture is not the same as a decision. When Asha drags a handle, she hasn't authorized anything — she's fired a macro. Authorization requires a moment of conscious approval. That became the organizing principle for the entire system.
Core pivot
02
A confidence score was replaced with the factors that produced it
TALON shows a probability percentage: "87% ignition risk." The operator either accepts it or doesn't, with nothing to evaluate. → TALON shows the contributing inputs: prior burn history for this grid, current wind vector, thermal delta above seasonal baseline. Something concrete to push back on.
A score gives the operator nothing to interrogate. If TALON is wrong, what exactly is wrong? Showing the inputs gives Asha a basis to override rather than blindly accept a number. Operators who understand the reasoning make better calls. And operators who override correctly build better trust over time.
AI governance
03
Authorizing deployment requires a 2-second press-hold, not a tap
A standard "Are you sure?" confirmation modal. Under time pressure, operators click through confirmations without reading them. → Press and hold for 2 seconds. Button shows progress, haptic pulse begins, fires on completion. Releasing early cancels with no action taken.
A tap feels proportional to sending a message, not to authorizing drone deployment over 47 occupied homes. Two seconds of sustained physical pressure makes the weight of the decision felt rather than just confirmed. The hold replaces a modal with a physical commitment that cannot be dismissed on reflex.
Commitment design
Designed for Failure

Four chaos states. The interface stays coherent when hardware fails, plans change, and TALON acts without waiting.

Every failure surfaces the problem as information rather than an alarm. The operator is already under pressure — a blocking error with no path forward is its own failure.

Signal degradation: blind drone
Smoke severs the camera feed mid-operation. TALON falls back to LiDAR and terrain reconstruction automatically. No blocking error. Asha loses video but keeps spatial awareness, drone position, and telemetry. The degraded state surfaces as a status chip in the left panel. Mission continues.
Mid-brief correction: voice input
TALON recommends Forest North; wind data has shifted. A Change Plan button in the modal footer opens a voice channel. Asha speaks the correction. TALON reprocesses inline, then presents the updated plan for confirmation before any drone moves.
Plan disagreement: override surface
Asha rejects the recommended containment plan. TALON had already pre-computed the alternate. She taps Override, selects a reason via pill chips, reviews the reshuffled plan, and authorizes from the same action bar. No re-entry. No blank fields. The override reason logs to the audit trail.
Autonomous handoff: battery 14%
Lidar-02 drops to 14% mid-rescue. TALON initiates the handoff before Asha can respond — a floating interrupt reads "RECALLING: Lidar-02. TAKING OVER: Scout-03." This is the one moment TALON acts without asking. Narration is immediate. She retains the option to stand down the entire mission.
What I'd Measure

Four metrics I'd track if this shipped.

Time to first decision
Core validation
From first alert to operator's first decision. If consistently above 15 seconds, either the classification output isn't clear or the alert modal isn't surfacing what it should.
Dismissed alert rate
Safety signal
What percentage of dismissed alerts turned into real incidents? Even a low rate here is a design problem. The ignition classification threshold needs tuning against actual outcomes, not assumptions.
Operator override rate
Trust calibration
How often does the operator modify the AI's proposed plan? Too low suggests rubber-stamping. Too high suggests the AI's proposals aren't trustworthy enough to act on as written. Target: 15–40% override rate.
Exception acknowledgment time
Interface check
How long do non-critical exceptions sit in the feed before someone acts? Long lag times suggest the feed is being ignored — which means the progressive density system may need adjustment.
Honest Reflection

What is a design estimate. Why the human gate is deliberate, not a compromise.

What needs validation
The 90-second target is design-estimated, not measured. I ran through the flow myself. I am not a trained dispatcher under operational pressure. Whether this holds under field conditions is what controlled testing with actual operators would close. I said that in the case study rather than presenting the estimate as validated data.
Why full autonomy was not the answer
Full autonomy would be faster. It would also mean drones deploying over residential areas at 3 AM based on a sensor that might be a controlled burn. FAA airspace rules, liability law, and emergency response protocols do not accommodate "the AI decided." The human gate is not friction. It is what makes this system legally operable.
If the system can secretly override Asha, Asha is the illusion of control. The interface has one job: make sure that never happens, and make sure Asha can always see that it hasn't.
