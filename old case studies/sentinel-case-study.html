TL;DR — For the scanners

The bottleneck in wildfire response isn't hardware. It's the 48-minute gap between detection and deployment — caused entirely by cognitive friction in the operator interface.

Sentinel redesigns that interface as a decision surface: AI handles triage, classification, and execution. The operator shows up at exactly the moments their judgment is required — nothing more, nothing less.

Core reframe
Not a dashboard. A decision surface.
Authority model
AI proposes. Operator authorizes. System executes.
Design constraint
No irreversible action without a human on record.
01 — Problem

The problem statement everyone gets wrong.

Drone hardware is fast enough. The interface isn't.

California wildfire response has a 48-minute average window from thermal detection to coordinated deployment. The instinct is to frame this as a sensor or drone speed problem. It isn't. The hardware exists. What's missing is an interface that can translate incoming sensor data into a deployment decision in under 90 seconds.

Operators in dispatch centers are receiving thermal alerts, satellite feeds, wind vectors, and ground sensor signals simultaneously — from disjointed systems. Under time pressure and sleep deprivation, they're manually assembling a threat picture. That assembly process is where the 48 minutes go.

The reframe: The problem isn't sensor coverage or drone speed. It's that no interface currently exists that handles the cognitive assembly work so operators can focus on the one thing AI can't do — make a judgment call.

02 — Information Architecture

Operator mental model mapped to system state.

Sentinel isn't a feature set — it's a state machine. The entire UI locks to one operational phase at a time. Panel content, action availability, and information density all shift based on where the operator is in the response. The IA isn't organized around what the system knows. It's organized around what the operator needs to do next.

03 — The 90-Second Window

What actually happens. Phase by phase.

Most people hear "48 minutes to 90 seconds" and imagine a single button press. The reality is a carefully sequenced handoff between what TALON does autonomously and what the operator consciously decides. Get that handoff wrong in either direction — operator drowning in tasks they shouldn't own, or AI making population-level decisions nobody authorised — and the system fails.

Scan → Alert
0 — 15 sec
TALON does
Thermal anomaly detected. Cross-references satellite IR, ground sensors, checks for registered controlled burns. Classifies ignition probability from historical burn data: prior events in this grid, current wind vector, seasonal thermal delta.

Dispatch drone to verify — or — Dismiss + notify ground team (fire dept / police)

Verify

04 — Design Decisions

Six pivots. Each one changed how the system thinks.

These aren't visual refinements. Each one represents a change in the underlying model of how the operator and the system should divide authority.

1
From forced navigation to interrupt design
Alert → context switch vs. alert → overlay
Before
Anomaly detected → entire UI snaps to a new Alert screen. The operator's spatial context is destroyed on every alert.
After
Alert arrives as a floating modal interrupt over the baseline map. The operator decides and responds without losing orientation.

Operators don't have "alert time" vs. "non-alert time." They're always mid-task. The interrupt model treats alerts as information that arrives alongside other work — not events that stop everything. It also clarified a key distinction we'd been conflating: an interrupt demands a decision on top of current state. A transition means the operator commits to a new phase. Once that was clear, every subsequent navigation decision became easier to make.

2
From replay verification to live signal classification
Trust through familiarity → trust through accuracy
Before
A "verification replay" clip — show the operator a short video to build confidence before deciding. Familiar. Reassuring. Wrong.
After
TALON classification: ignition probability scored from historical burn data for this specific grid. Inputs surfaced alongside the score — not hidden behind it.

Fire doesn't have a replay. Detection comes from thermal sensors and satellite signals. There's no video record of the anomaly appearing — we were borrowing a consumer product mental model. The deeper problem: we were designing trust through familiarity rather than accuracy. Operators don't need something reassuring. They need something that's true. Showing the reasoning — prior burn events, current wind vector, thermal delta above baseline — gives the operator the basis to agree or push back. A percentage alone gives them nothing to work with.

3
Progressive information density
Constant ~120 elements → density scales inversely with urgency
Before
Same UI density across all phases. ~120 text elements in Contain. Paragraphs of rationale during the moment of maximum urgency.
After
Scan = verbose context. Contain = headlines + status indicators. Rescue = zero prose, large numbers, one log entry, exception signals only.

Under stress, humans process numbers and spatial cues faster than prose. Every panel component needed three render states tied to phase. The Intel panel in Scan is a different component from the Intel panel in Rescue — same position, different contract with the operator.

4
Action bar as a curated timeline
All actions visible → consequence-tiered, secondary behind tray
Before
All actions in one scrolling row. Administrative tasks (Notify Authorities) adjacent to irreversible actions (Emergency Evacuate).
After
Primary phase-appropriate actions in main row. Secondary / administrative behind a chevron tray. In Rescue, the primary CTA is absent — its absence signals execution, not inaction.

Proximity creates accidental authority. In a crisis, the cognitive cost of avoiding a destructive action you can see is always present. The bar communicates system state through what's on it and what isn't — the empty Rescue action bar is itself a signal that the system is executing correctly.

5
Eliminating free-text from critical decisions
Type "Route A, 15 personnel" → team chips + zone assignment
Before
Evacuation modal with free-text HTML inputs. Operator types the route, types a personnel count. Typing under stress is error-prone. "15 personnel" is not how ground teams work.
After
Role-based team chips (Alpha Lead, Bravo Team). Operator assigns team to zone. System filters valid zones by team certification. Team assignments made in Contain — evacuation modal is review, not first-time decision.

Typing during a stress spike forces the operator to translate a spatial reality into words. The pre-staging logic is the more important change: by the time the operator reaches Rescue, team assignments are already authorized. The evacuation confirmation becomes a review of a staged plan, not a decision under maximum pressure.

6
From anonymous AI to TALON — accountable system agent
"AI Confidence 84%" → TALON: classified high ignition risk
Before
"AI Confidence," "AI Recommends," "AI Map." Generic. Black-box. No way to attribute or review AI actions.
After
TALON. Every autonomous action logged under the TALON callsign with a timestamp — separated from operator actions in the audit trail. Cyan in the activity log.

Operators in life-critical environments are appropriately skeptical of black-box systems. The naming isn't branding — it's an accountability mechanism. TALON can be audited. "AI" cannot. The nomenclature shift extended across the full interface: Activity Log (not History), Anomaly Validation (not Check Fire), TALON Spatial Reconstruction (not AI Map). The language signals institutional accountability, not a feature recommendation engine.

05 — Final Design

Four operator modes. One persistent spatial canvas.

The final system is a three-panel layout that holds across all five phases. Left: Intel / Corroboration panel — content updates based on zone selection or active incident context. Center: 3D terrain canvas — photorealistic base layer (reality) with 2D vector intelligence overlaid (TALON outputs). Right: Priority Risks / AI Next Steps — a decision queue ranked by consequence, not time.

Screen 04
Rescue
Execution mode. Structure clearance tracking. Exception feed only.
04 / Rescue System executes. Drone guidance active on evacuation routes. Ground teams confirmed on-scene via trackers. Structure clearance count live. No primary CTA — its absence signals correct execution.
The visual hierarchy rule: Zone boundaries are the brightest map elements. Drone patrol routes are faint cyan at 20% opacity in baseline state. Spread zones activate at Verify and increase in visual weight as urgency escalates. Reality layer (3D terrain) and intelligence layer (2D vectors) are deliberately separated — one photorealistic, one crisp geometry. Neither competes for the same depth plane.
06 — Reflection

What I got wrong. What I'd measure.

The 90-second claim is a testable hypothesis, not a guaranteed outcome. The actual bottleneck may not be the interface at all — it may be radio communication protocols between dispatch and field units, or network latency in high-smoke environments where drone feeds degrade. I designed for the interface variable without validating whether it's the binding constraint.

The TALON classification model surfaces historical ignition probability. But terrain reconstruction accuracy under active fire conditions is genuinely hard — smoke, thermal noise, and rapidly changing perimeters will degrade model confidence in exactly the moments it matters most. The design doesn't yet surface this degradation gracefully.

What I'd measure: Time from alert interrupt to operator decision (dispatch / dismiss). Decision accuracy — dismissed alerts that became incidents. Operator intervention rate during execution (lower is not always better — zero interventions may mean the exception feed is suppressing real issues).

The most honest thing I can say about this project: I started designing screens. I ended up designing a decision-making system. The two things are not the same, and most of the pivots documented here are the specific moments I noticed the difference.

The interface should disappear during action. What remains should be exactly the decision the operator needs to make.
