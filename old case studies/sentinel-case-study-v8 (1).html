01 — The Problem

The hardware was fast. The interface wasn't.

FlytBase builds autonomous drone infrastructure for public safety. Their drones can launch without a pilot, stream live thermal footage, and coordinate across a whole fleet without any human input. In a wildfire scenario, that hardware can get a drone airborne and feeding live thermal data within 30 seconds of detection. Getting from sensor alert to coordinated response in 90 seconds was already within reach — technically.

Their Design Fusion S3 challenge gave me a specific brief: design the command center interface that makes those 90 seconds real. Not a concept. Not a dashboard. A structured operator experience sitting between the autonomous hardware and the human who has to act on it — in the dark, at 3 AM, while managing multiple zones at once.

The 48-minute baseline makes the design problem concrete. The sensor fires instantly. The drone can be airborne in under a minute. But between alert and decision, there's an 11-minute gap where the operator manually cross-references three disconnected systems (satellite feed, sensor dashboard, weather monitor) and assembles a picture the interface should have built for them. That's not a hardware failure. That's the missing interface layer.

The cognitive load is the bottleneck. The data exists. The tools exist. The operator's brain — doing assembly work that the interface should be doing — is what keeps 48 minutes possible when 90 seconds is not.

02 — Research

What I read — and the four things that actually changed the design.

I did desk research, not field research. I read about emergency dispatch workflows, how operators behave under time pressure, and how trust works in human-AI systems for critical decisions. No real wildfire dispatchers were interviewed; I'm being upfront about that. But four findings from that research directly shaped every design decision that followed.

01
Operators build the picture manually. Today's dispatch tools don't talk to each other. The operator pulls from separate sensor dashboards, satellite feeds, and weather monitors. The mental model is theirs to assemble alone.
02
More information at the wrong moment makes things worse. Under stress, people fixate on one data point. Flooding the screen with more data at peak urgency slows decisions down — it doesn't speed them up.
03
Operators approve plans — they don't pilot assets. The mental model of "operator as pilot" breaks down at scale. What they're actually doing is deciding whether a proposed plan is right — not controlling individual drones.
04
Every decision needs to be traceable. In a critical system, you can't have ambiguity about whether a human or AI made a call. Legal accountability and operational trust both depend on a clear, permanent record.

Finding 1 → design decisions 1 and 3. Finding 2 → progressive density system. Finding 3 → the scope boundary between TALON and Asha. Finding 4 → the TALON callsign and audit trail in Section 04.

03 — What I Got Wrong First

My first version solved the wrong problem.

Finding 2 sent me in the wrong direction. More information at the wrong moment makes decisions slower, so the obvious answer seemed to be compressing the interface as much as possible. That thinking produced the Escalation Slider.

04 — Design Decisions

Six decisions that shaped the final system.

These aren't aesthetic choices. Each one changed something about how the system and the operator share responsibility. The before/after shows what changed; the reasoning explains why it mattered.

1
Alerts navigated to a new screen → alerts float over the current one
Jumping to a new screen every alert wiped out the spatial context Asha was already working with.
Before
Alert fires → full UI switches to a new screen. Whatever Asha was watching disappears.
After
Alert arrives as a modal over the current map. Her spatial context stays. She decides without losing her bearings.

Operators are always mid-task. A screen switch on every alert forces a full context reset. The modal keeps Asha's spatial bearings intact; TALON folds to the left panel while she returns to whatever she was doing.

2
Confidence percentage → showing the reasoning behind it
A score alone gives the operator nothing to push back on. Showing the inputs does.
Before
A short "verification replay" clip. Borrowed from consumer product thinking. Fire detection doesn't have a replay.
After
TALON shows its inputs — prior burn history for this grid, current wind vector, thermal delta above seasonal baseline — not just the conclusion.

If TALON says "high ignition risk" and Asha disagrees, she needs something to push back on. A score gives her nothing. The contributing inputs give her the basis to agree or to override.

3
Same density across every phase → information scales with urgency
120 elements during active rescue. Reading paragraphs of AI reasoning at peak stress is the opposite of reducing cognitive load.

4
All actions visible at once → only the right actions shown per phase
"Notify Authorities" next to "Emergency Evacuate" at the same visual weight. When everything looks equal, nothing is urgent.

Each phase shows only the actions relevant to that moment. In Scan, the action bar is empty — the system is monitoring, nothing to decide. In Contain, a two-second hold replaces the "are you sure?" modal: the physical commitment matches the weight of the decision. In Rescue, there is no primary action at all. An absent button is the signal the system is executing correctly. Screenshots below show each state individually.

5
Typing during an emergency → team chips selected before the emergency peaks
Typing "Route A, 15 personnel" at maximum stress isn't just slow — it's the wrong model for how ground teams actually work.
Before
Free-text inputs in evacuation modal. Type the route. Type a headcount. "15 personnel" isn't how real ground teams are organized.
After
Team chips (Alpha Lead, Bravo, Charlie) assigned to zones in Contain — before Rescue starts. By the time Asha hits Rescue, she's just confirming a plan she already approved.

Once the plan is assembled with the right teams in the right zones, a simple tap isn't enough to execute it. The commitment mechanism needs to match the weight of the decision.

6
Calling it "AI" → giving it a name, a callsign, and a log entry
You can't audit "AI." You can audit TALON.

Calling it "AI" meant no one could hold it accountable. TALON is a callsign — its actions log in cyan, Asha's in white, always visually separated. Anyone reviewing the audit trail can see exactly what the system did and what the operator decided. Naming it turned a black box into a recognisable partner with a consistent, auditable behaviour.

05 — Design System

Every visual choice connects back to the problem.

This is a dark interface because Sentinel is a night-shift tool. A bright UI at 3:47 AM in a darkened ops center would actively degrade Asha's vision and wash out the thermal map. Every choice — the phase-color system escalating from teal through deep red, TALON's actions always in cyan and Asha's always in white, tabular numerics on every timer — answers the same question as the design decisions: what does the operator need to process faster?

07 — The Map

The map and the UI are two separate things. That separation is what makes both work.

There's a common trap in command center design: make the map do everything. Render the drones, the zones, the alerts, all on top of each other. The result looks like a cluttered war room and reads like one too. The operator can't tell what's real environment and what's system output.

Sentinel separates them cleanly. The 3D terrain base is reality — photorealistic California foothills, fire volume, the physical environment as it actually exists. The 2D vector layer floating above it is what TALON is doing about it: drone positions, zone boundaries, spread estimates as sharp, scalable geometry. At 4 AM, Asha can look at the map and immediately know: the glowing terrain is what's happening, the cyan crosshairs are what the system has deployed. No decoding required.

Something that only broke in testing

Early prototype: standard proportional fonts on all countdown timers. Looked clean in Figma. In actual testing, the numbers jumped sideways every second — wide digits like 0 and 8 swapping with narrow ones like 1. You don't see it in a static mockup. After an hour of watching it on a live system, it causes eye strain.

Fix: font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums on every timer. Every digit takes the same horizontal space regardless of shape. Baseline stays fixed. Small detail, real ergonomic consequence.
08 — When Things Go Wrong

What happens when the system doesn't work cleanly.

A wildfire at 4 AM doesn't give you clean inputs. Smoke kills camera feeds. Two sensors contradict each other. Three things fail at once. I designed for these scenarios late, but they're where the most honest design thinking happened. How a system handles failure tells you more about its design than how it handles success.

📡
Chaos 01 · Hardware
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
What happens
Dense smoke severs camera feeds at exactly the moments they're needed. Most systems: "Connection Lost" modal, gap in map.
TALON fallback
No blocking error. TALON falls back to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction automatically. Asha loses video — keeps spatial awareness, drone position, telemetry. Degraded state surfaces as a status chip in the left panel. Mission continues.
⚠️
Chaos 02 · AI Contradiction
Contradicting signals — when TALON says "I don't know"
What happens
Satellite reads "Active fire" at Grid 4C. Ground sensor reads "Clear." Two authoritative sources, directly contradicting.
Designed response
TALON doesn't synthesize a false score. Both conflicting signals surface side by side. Recommendation field intentionally blank. Priority card: "Conflicting source data — operator judgment required." Asha breaks the tie. An AI that forces a recommendation when it doesn't know is more dangerous than one that admits uncertainty.

Chaos 03 · Real-Time Data Shift
Mid-decision confidence flip
What happens
Asha is hovering over AUTHORIZE at 88% confidence. Wind shifts. TALON's confidence drops to 41% in 3 seconds. Decision in motion, interrupted by invalidating data.
Designed response
No blocking error modal — a panic modal at this moment causes worse decisions. Instead: AUTHORIZE button downgrades from amber to disabled grey instantly. Chaos 04 · Compound Failure
Confidence drops + drone fails + second alert fires simultaneously
What happens
At 03:49:12, 21 seconds into Rescue: confidence drops to 39% (wind shift), Scout-02 loses feed (smoke), second alert fires in Grid 6A. Three concurrent failures during the phase with no primary action button.
Designed response
Exception Feed stacks events by consequence, not arrival time. Scout-02's degradation becomes a LiDAR fallback silently — chip, not modal. Confidence drop downgrades action bar state visually. Rejected
The System Override. System secretly re-activates containment without telling Asha.

09 — What I'd Do Differently

Honest about what's unfinished — and what's intentional.

What "90 seconds" actually means

The 90-second target is detection to authorized response handoff — not full evacuation, not every drone on station, not 47 households cleared. The clock ends when TALON has delivered a coordinated briefing package to the fire department and Asha has authorized the containment plan. Everything after that is response execution time, and that isn't a design problem Sentinel can solve.

The brief's own scenario makes this clear: fire department receives GPS coordinates, thermal map, and approach route at the 108-second mark. Evacuation alerts go out at 3:49:05. The brief itself doesn't hit 90 seconds to full response. It hits 90 seconds to the decision and handoff that makes a coordinated response possible. That's the target Sentinel is designed for.

Why the human stays in the loop — a deliberate choice, not a limitation

Full autonomy would be faster. It would also mean drones deploying over a residential area at 3 AM based entirely on a thermal sensor reading that might be a controlled burn, a faulty sensor, or a false pattern match. No human saw it. No one authorized it. When something goes wrong — and at scale, it will — there's no accountability chain and no legal clarity.

The regulatory reality alone makes full autonomy non-deployable today. FAA airspace rules, liability law, and municipal emergency response protocols don't accommodate "the AI decided and launched." The human gate in Sentinel isn't friction introduced by a cautious designer. It's the thing that makes this system legally operable and auditable in a real emergency.

This is also why TALON proposes and Asha authorizes — every time, without exception. As TALON's models improve on real incident data and operator override patterns reveal which decisions are consistently approved unchanged, the authority boundary can shift. But that delegation needs to be earned through a track record, not assumed from day one. The audit trail Sentinel generates now is the training signal that makes safer delegation possible later.

Edge cases the 90-second window doesn't survive

Drone dock distance is the biggest variable. If the nearest dock is 4 kilometers from Grid 4C, the Verify phase alone blows the window before Asha makes a single decision. Heavy network latency in smoke-filled terrain degrades TALON's sensor fusion. An operator already managing a concurrent active incident can't give a new alert full attention in the first 15 seconds. These aren't design failures — they're infrastructure and operational constraints the interface can surface but can't control. The chaos states section exists precisely because the interface needs to stay coherent when those constraints hit mid-response.

On validating the 90-second target

I ran through the flow myself and estimated that a trained operator, familiar with TALON's classification language and the hold gesture, would clear the three decision points in roughly 90 seconds under normal conditions. That's an assumption, not a measurement. I'm not a trained dispatcher, and I'm not under real operational pressure. The honest version of this claim is: the interface is designed so that the decisions themselves take seconds, not minutes, when the operator knows the system. Whether that holds under field conditions is what controlled testing with actual operators would need to close.

If this shipped, I'd measure: Time from first alert to operator decision. How often dismissed alerts became real incidents. Operator override rate — too low might mean Asha is rubber-stamping plans she hasn't fully reviewed. How long non-critical exceptions sit in the feed before someone acknowledges them.

"I started designing screens. I ended up thinking about decision-making systems. Those aren't the same thing — and every section of this case study is the moment I noticed the difference."

Good interface design disappears when you need it most. What's left is exactly the decision worth making.
