01 — The Problem

The hardware was fast. The interface wasn't.

FlytBase builds autonomous drone infrastructure for public safety. Their drones can launch without a pilot, stream live thermal footage, and coordinate across a whole fleet without any human input. In a wildfire scenario, that hardware can get a drone airborne and feeding live thermal data within 30 seconds of detection. Getting from sensor alert to coordinated response in 90 seconds was already within reach — technically.

Their Design Fusion S3 challenge gave me a specific brief: design the command center interface that makes those 90 seconds real. Not a concept. Not a dashboard. A structured operator experience sitting between the autonomous hardware and the human who has to act on it — in the dark, at 3 AM, while managing multiple zones at once.

The 48-minute baseline makes the design problem concrete. The sensor fires instantly. The drone can be airborne in under a minute. But between alert and decision, there's an 11-minute gap where the operator manually cross-references three disconnected systems (satellite feed, sensor dashboard, weather monitor) and assembles a picture the interface should have built for them. That's not a hardware failure. That's the missing interface layer.

The cognitive load is the bottleneck. The data exists. The tools exist. The operator's brain, doing assembly work the interface should handle, is what keeps 48 minutes possible when 90 seconds is not.

The specific problem: Emergency operators fail to authorize containment within the first 10 minutes of confirmed detection because command tools require manually cross-referencing 4-6 disconnected data streams. The result: 48 minutes from alert to first drone deployment on average. In dry-season terrain, fire spreads at 0.3-0.8 hectares per minute inside that window.

Look at the timeline above. The sensor fires instantly. The drone can be airborne in under a minute. But between the alert arriving and a decision being made, there's an 11-minute gap: the operator cross-referencing three disconnected systems by hand. That 11 minutes is the interface failure. It's the work Sentinel exists to absorb.

Sentinel is the interface layer between FlytBase's autonomous hardware and the operator. Instead of the operator assembling a picture from disconnected sensor logs, Sentinel does the data fusion, corroboration, and situation modeling — so that by the time they're interrupted, a pre-built situation summary is already waiting. Their job becomes three explicit decisions. Everything else is handled.
02 — Research

What I read — and the four things that actually changed the design.

I did desk research, not field research. I read about emergency dispatch workflows, how operators behave under time pressure, and how trust works in human-AI systems for critical decisions. No real wildfire dispatchers were interviewed; I'm being upfront about that. But four findings from that research directly shaped every design decision that followed.

01
Operators build the picture manually. Today's dispatch tools don't talk to each other. The operator pulls from separate sensor dashboards, satellite feeds, and weather monitors. The mental model is theirs to assemble alone.
02
More information at the wrong moment makes things worse. Under stress, people fixate on one data point. Flooding the screen with more data at peak urgency slows decisions down — it doesn't speed them up.
03
Operators approve plans — they don't pilot assets. The mental model of "operator as pilot" breaks down at scale. What they're actually doing is deciding whether a proposed plan is right — not controlling individual drones.
04
Every decision needs to be traceable. In a critical system, you can't have ambiguity about whether a human or AI made a call. Legal accountability and operational trust both depend on a clear, permanent record.

Finding 1 → design decisions 1 and 3. Finding 2 → progressive density system. Finding 3 → the scope boundary between TALON and Asha. Finding 4 → the TALON callsign and audit trail in Section 04.

03 — What I Got Wrong First

My first version solved the wrong problem.

Finding 2 sent me in the wrong direction. More information at the wrong moment makes decisions slower, so the obvious answer seemed to be compressing the interface as much as possible. That thinking produced the Escalation Slider.

Early wireframe · V0.1

One control. Drag from Assess → Contain → Rescue. Each position triggers a pre-set AI script — drones deploy, notifications go out, zones get classified. Minimal UI. Maximum speed.

The problem: dragging a slider isn't making a decision. Drag to Contain, and drones deploy to positions Asha never reviewed. The audit trail just reads "operator moved slider at 03:48" — nobody authorized anything, a gesture fired a macro. The wireframes had a second issue too: roughly 120 text elements per phase. I was trying to reduce cognitive load while actively adding to it.

Rejected
Escalation Slider — drag triggers AI macros across three predefined states.
Chosen
AI proposes a specific plan. Operator reads it and explicitly approves. Every single time.
Why
A gesture isn't a decision. If the system acts on a macro, the operator didn't authorize anything — they just moved a handle.

04 — Design Decisions

Six decisions that shaped the final system.

Interface anatomyComponent labels — panels, action bar, TALON outputs, and audit log.

These aren't aesthetic choices. Each one changed something about how the system and the operator share responsibility. The before/after shows what changed; the reasoning explains why it mattered.

1
Alerts navigated to a new screen → alerts float over the current one
Jumping to a new screen every alert wiped out the spatial context Asha was already working with.
Before
Alert fires → full UI switches to a new screen. Whatever Asha was watching disappears.
After
Alert arrives as a modal over the current map. Her spatial context stays. She decides without losing her bearings.

Operators are always mid-task when an alert fires. A screen switch costs 4-8 seconds of spatial reorientation, time that doesn't exist in the Verify phase. The modal keeps Asha's geographic bearings intact; TALON folds to the left panel while she returns to what she was watching. The tradeoff: the alert is visually quieter than a full-screen interrupt. A missed alert is recoverable. A containment decision made without spatial context is not.

4
All actions visible at once → only the right actions shown per phase
"Notify Authorities" next to "Emergency Evacuate" at the same visual weight. When everything looks equal, nothing is urgent.

Each phase shows only the actions relevant to that moment. In Scan, the action bar is empty. In Contain, a two-second hold replaces the confirmation modal; the physical commitment matches the weight of the decision. In Rescue, there's no primary action at all. An absent button is the signal the system is executing correctly. The tradeoff: operators can't shortcut phases regardless of experience. Optimizing for the trained expert would cost the majority their safety margin, and the metric is time-to-authorization under stress, not under familiarity.

6
Calling it "AI" → giving it a name, a callsign, and a log entry
You can't audit "AI." You can audit TALON.

Calling it "AI" meant no one could hold it accountable. TALON is a callsign — its actions log in cyan, Asha's in white, always visually separated. Anyone reviewing the audit trail can see exactly what the system did and what the operator decided. Naming it turned a black box into a recognisable partner with a consistent, auditable behaviour.

06 — Visual Architecture: Engineering "Visual Silence"

The map and the UI are two separate things. That separation is what makes both work.

The map is Reality — 3D. Photorealistic terrain, fire volume, wind-constrained topography. This layer communicates physical threat viscerally. The UI is Intelligence — 2D. Drone positions, zone boundaries, spread estimates as sharp scalable vectors floating above. Neither layer competes for the same visual depth plane — operator can instantly distinguish physical environment from system proposals.

This separation directly enables the smoke degradation fallback: when optical feed is lost, TALON switches to LiDAR — Asha loses video without losing spatial awareness. The Intelligence layer is fully independent of the camera feed.

07 — When Things Go Wrong

What happens when the system doesn't work cleanly.

A wildfire at 4 AM doesn't give you clean inputs. Smoke kills camera feeds. Two sensors contradict each other. Three things fail at once. I designed for these scenarios late, but they're where the most honest design thinking happened. How a system handles failure tells you more about its design than how it handles success.

📡
Chaos 01 · Hardware
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
What happens
Dense smoke severs camera feeds at exactly the moments they're needed. Most systems: "Connection Lost" modal, gap in map.
TALON fallback
No blocking error. TALON falls back to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction automatically. Asha loses video — keeps spatial awareness, drone position, telemetry. Degraded state surfaces as a status chip in the left panel. Mission continues.

Chaos 02 · Mid-Brief Correction
Operator needs to adjust a mission after TALON presents the brief
What happens
The Mission Brief modal opens for "Stage Perimeter." TALON recommends Forest North at 40m AGL. Asha can see the wind data has shifted since the plan was generated. She needs to relocate the hold to Buffer East and drop altitude — but the modal only shows Approve or Cancel.
Designed response
A voice input channel built into the brief itself. A "🎙 Change Plan" button sits in the modal footer alongside Cancel and Approve & Deploy. Asha clicks it and speaks the correction. TALON reprocesses in real-time — the modal transitions through listening (waveform animation), processing (recomputing), and responded states inline. The badge updates to "TALON Revised Plan · Operator Override," the rationale text rewrites to reflect the correction, and the confirm button becomes "Deploy Revised Plan" in amber. The operator reviews what TALON understood before a single drone moves. Voice input needs a review step before execution — TALON never acts on a voice command it hasn't confirmed back.

Chaos 03 · Plan Disagreement
Operator rejects TALON's recommended containment plan
What happens
TALON proposes deploying to Forest North first. Asha knows the wind shifted northeast — the buffer corridor is now the correct anchor. The recommended plan is wrong. She needs to change it without losing time or re-entering parameters from scratch.
Designed response
An alternate plan surface, not a form. TALON has already pre-computed the buffer-first scenario before Asha ever saw the recommendation. She taps Override, selects a reason via pill chips (False positive / Incomplete data / Operational constraint / Other), reviews the alternate plan summary — zone priorities reshuffled, drone positions adjusted — and authorizes from the same action bar. No blank fields. No re-entry. The AI had both options ready before she asked. The override reason is logged to the audit trail so that TALON's future recommendations can learn which proposals operators consistently correct.

Chaos 04 · Autonomous Handoff
Drone hits 14% battery mid-rescue — TALON acts without waiting
What happens
Lidar-02 is holding the perimeter monitor position during active rescue. Battery drops to 14%. There is no time for Asha to manually reassign coverage — the perimeter gap would be immediate.
Designed response
TALON initiates the handoff autonomously — then narrates it. A floating interrupt card appears over the map (not replacing it — Asha keeps full spatial awareness). Two-column layout: "RECALLING: Lidar-02 · 14% · RTB" and "TAKING OVER: Scout-03 · En route · ETA 00:42." The perimeter drone begins its return. The substitute is already moving. Asha didn't manage this — she reads it happening. This is the one moment TALON acts without asking. The design rule: autonomous battery handoffs are mechanical decisions the system makes faster than a human can. But the narration is immediate and the operator retains the option to stand down the entire mission if the handoff doesn't satisfy her.

Infrastructure failures escalate through three severity grades. Network degraded (340ms latency): thin yellow banner, operations valid, feeds may arrive late, nothing locks. Satellite feed offline: amber banner with a hard gate — AUTHORIZE is disabled until the link restores, because authorizing containment without spatial corroboration is a decision the system won't accelerate. Total infrastructure failure — TALON offline, satellite gone, all drone telemetry timed out — replaces the entire interface with a full-screen manual protocol: the fallback radio channel, the regional dispatch landline number, the last known system state, and a single restart button. Yellow means watch it. Amber means you're constrained. A red full-screen border means put down the interface and pick up the radio.

08 — Projected Business Impact

What 46 minutes earlier actually means.

The calculation

The interface reduces operator decision lag from 48 minutes to 90 seconds per incident: 46 minutes of earlier containment authorization per callout. Forest Survey of India's biannual State of Forest Reports document tens of thousands of forest fire incidents annually across India. For a system deployed in high-severity zones, early containment authorization consistently reduces the aerial resources required per incident. NFPA initial attack effectiveness research shows fires contained within the first 30 minutes require significantly fewer sorties than fires contained after the first hour. Earlier authorization means a smaller initial burn radius. A smaller burn radius means fewer resources, fewer at-risk structures, and a shorter total response. The time savings compound into operational savings that grow with every minute the system runs faster than the status quo.

Methodology: Formula 03 (time savings at scale). Incident volume basis from Forest Survey of India State of Forest Reports. Resource reduction principle from NFPA initial attack effectiveness research. Projected figures, not post-launch measurements.

Why the human stays in the loop

Full autonomy would be faster. It would also mean drones deploying over a residential area at 3 AM based on a thermal sensor reading that could be a controlled burn or a false match. When something goes wrong at scale, there's no accountability chain and no legal clarity.

FAA airspace rules, liability law, and municipal emergency response protocols don't accommodate "the AI decided and launched." The human gate in Sentinel isn't cautious design. It's what makes the system legally operable in a real emergency. TALON proposes, Asha authorizes, every time, without exception. That boundary is the audit trail. The audit trail is the only thing that makes future delegation possible.

What's next

The TALON override rate is the metric I'd instrument first. If operators approve recommendations without modification more than 85% of the time, the system is producing rubber-stamp behavior, a safety risk dressed as efficiency. If the override rate exceeds 40%, TALON's training data doesn't reflect real field conditions. That number tells you whether the AI is earning its authority or just borrowing it.
