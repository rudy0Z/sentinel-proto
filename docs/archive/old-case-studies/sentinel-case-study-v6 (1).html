01 — The Problem

The hardware was fast. The interface wasn't.

FlytBase builds autonomous drone infrastructure for public safety. Their drones can launch without a pilot, stream live thermal footage, and coordinate across a whole fleet. For a wildfire scenario, all of this can happen in under two minutes.

So why did emergency response still take 48 minutes? That was the question at the centre of their Design Fusion S3 challenge.

The answer isn't in the hardware. It's in the gap between the alert firing and the operator feeling confident enough to act. When a thermal anomaly comes in at 3:47 AM, the operator has to manually pull data from three separate systems — a satellite feed, a sensor dashboard, and a weather monitor — piece together what's happening, figure out how serious it is, and then decide whether to deploy. That process takes time. Not because they're slow, but because no system is doing that assembly for them.

That's the cognitive load problem. The data exists. The tools exist. The operator's brain is the bottleneck — and the interface wasn't designed to reduce that load at all.

Look at the timeline above. The sensor fires instantly. The drone can be airborne in under a minute. But between the alert arriving and a decision being made, there's an 11-minute gap — the operator cross-referencing three disconnected systems by hand.

What I set out to design: An interface that does the data assembly for the operator — so by the time they're interrupted, the picture is already built. They're not reading raw sensor logs. They're looking at a pre-corroborated, spatially organized situation summary and making one decision: is this real, and should I respond?

02 — Research

What I read — and the four things that actually changed the design.

I did desk research, not field research. I read about emergency dispatch workflows, how operators behave under time pressure, and how trust works in human-AI systems for critical decisions. No real wildfire dispatchers were interviewed — I'm being upfront about that. But four findings from that research directly shaped every design decision that followed.

01
Operators build the picture manually. Today's dispatch tools don't talk to each other. The operator pulls from separate sensor dashboards, satellite feeds, and weather monitors. The mental model is theirs to assemble alone.
02
More information at the wrong moment makes things worse. Under stress, people fixate on one data point. Flooding the screen with more data at peak urgency slows decisions down — it doesn't speed them up.
03
Operators approve plans — they don't pilot assets. The mental model of "operator as pilot" breaks down at scale. What they're actually doing is deciding whether a proposed plan is right — not controlling individual drones.
04
Every decision needs to be traceable. In a critical system, you can't have ambiguity about whether a human or AI made a call. Legal accountability and operational trust both depend on a clear, permanent record.

Finding 1 → design pivots 1 and 3. Finding 2 → progressive density system. Finding 3 → the scope boundary between TALON and Asha. Finding 4 → the TALON callsign and audit trail in Section 06.

03 — What I Got Wrong First

My first version solved the wrong problem.

Finding #2 — more information at the wrong moment makes decisions slower — sent me in the wrong direction. If cognitive overload is the problem, the obvious answer is to compress the interface as much as possible. That thinking produced the Escalation Slider.

Operators in high-stakes environments don't trust black boxes — they trust systems that behave predictably and can be reviewed after the fact. TALON is built on four principles that earn that trust through behaviour, not branding:

Transparency
Shows why it made a choice — inputs, not just conclusion.
Consistency
Layout never jumps. Spatial memory works under stress.
Accountability
TALON signs every action in the log. Always distinguishable from operator actions.
Reversibility
Every AI proposal has a one-tap Override. If it can't be undone, it can't be trusted.

06 — Design System

Every visual choice connects back to the problem.

This isn't a dark-mode interface because it looks cool. It's dark because Sentinel is a night-shift tool — 3:47 AM in a darkened ops center. A bright UI would actively degrade Asha's vision and wash out the thermal map. The color choices, the typography, the phase accents — all of it answers the same question as the design decisions: what does the operator need to process faster?

08 — The Map

The map and the UI are two separate things. That separation is what makes both work.

There's a common trap in command center design: make the map do everything — render the drones, the zones, the alerts, all on top of each other. The result looks like a cluttered war room and reads like one too. The operator can't tell what's real environment and what's system output.

Sentinel separates them cleanly. The 3D terrain base is reality — photorealistic California foothills, fire volume, the physical environment as it actually exists. The 2D vector layer floating above it is what TALON is doing about it — drone positions, zone boundaries, spread estimates as sharp, scalable geometry. At 4 AM, Asha can look at the map and immediately know: the glowing terrain is what's happening, the cyan crosshairs are what the system has deployed. No decoding required.

Something that only broke in testing

Early prototype: standard proportional fonts on all countdown timers. Looked clean in Figma. In actual testing, the numbers jumped sideways every second — wide digits like 0 and 8 swapping with narrow ones like 1. You don't see it in a static mockup. After an hour of watching it on a live system, it causes eye strain.

Fix: font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums on every timer. Every digit takes the same horizontal space regardless of shape. Baseline stays fixed. Small detail, real ergonomic consequence.
09 — When Things Go Wrong

What happens when the system doesn't work cleanly.

A wildfire at 4 AM doesn't give you clean inputs. Smoke kills camera feeds. Two sensors contradict each other. Three things fail at once. I designed for these scenarios late — but they're where the most honest design thinking happened. How a system handles failure tells you more about its design than how it handles success.

📡
Chaos 01 · Hardware
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
What happens
Dense smoke severs camera feeds at exactly the moments they're needed. Most systems: "Connection Lost" modal, gap in map.
TALON fallback
No blocking error. TALON falls back to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction automatically. Asha loses video — keeps spatial awareness, drone position, telemetry. Degraded state surfaces as a status chip in the left panel. Mission continues.
⚠️
Chaos 02 · AI Contradiction
Contradicting signals — when TALON says "I don't know"
What happens
Satellite reads "Active fire" at Grid 4C. Ground sensor reads "Clear." Two authoritative sources, directly contradicting.
Designed response
TALON doesn't synthesize a false score. Both conflicting signals surface side by side. Recommendation field intentionally blank. Priority card: "Conflicting source data — operator judgment required." Asha breaks the tie. An AI that forces a recommendation when it doesn't know is more dangerous than one that admits uncertainty.

What happens

No blocking error modal — a panic modal at this moment causes worse decisions. Instead: AUTHORIZE button downgrades from amber to disabled grey instantly. 💥
Chaos 04 · Compound Failure
Confidence drops + drone fails + second alert fires simultaneously
What happens
At 03:49:12, 21 seconds into Rescue: confidence drops to 39% (wind shift), Scout-02 loses feed (smoke), second alert fires in Grid 6A. Three concurrent failures during the phase with no primary action button.
Designed response
Exception Feed stacks events by consequence, not arrival time. Scout-02's degradation becomes a LiDAR fallback silently — chip, not modal. Confidence drop downgrades action bar state visually. Grid 6A alert queues with TALON pre-assessment ready — cannot stack over active Rescue phase. The system serialises the chaos so the operator doesn't have to.
Design decision · Operator dismisses an alert — fire spreads anyway
Rejected
The System Override. System secretly re-activates containment without telling Asha.
Chosen
Re-Escalation as New Event. Spread triggers a "Critical Update" interrupt — fresh, conscious decision required.
Why
If the system can secretly override Asha, Asha is the illusion of control. The system can challenge her with new data. It cannot steal her authority.
Last resort: ABORT MISSION — always in the action bar, always one tap, always the same position, no confirmation modal. When something is catastrophically wrong, Asha should not be hunting through menus.
10 — What I'd Do Differently

Honest about what's unfinished.

The 90-second window is a design target, not a validated outcome. The real bottleneck might not be the interface at all — it could be radio protocols between dispatch and field units, or network latency in smoke-heavy terrain. I designed for the interface problem without proving it's the binding constraint. That's the gap field testing would need to close.

The part of the system I'm least confident in is the override flow — when Asha disagrees with TALON's plan and takes manual control. I designed the clean version of that interaction. I never tested it with a real dispatcher under time pressure. That's exactly where the system is most likely to fail, and exactly where I'd spend a second iteration.

If this shipped, I'd measure: Time from first alert to operator decision. How often dismissed alerts became real incidents. Operator override rate — too low might mean Asha is rubber-stamping plans she hasn't really reviewed. How long non-critical exceptions sit in the feed before someone acknowledges them.

Next version would handle multiple incidents simultaneously — parallel fires, parallel command contexts, operator handoffs mid-crisis. The architecture could support it. The interface isn't there yet. And eventually, full state-of-emergency mode — multiple crises at once, cross-agency coordination. Zombie-apocalypse scenario. That's the problem I'd actually want to tackle next.

Where this goes from here — more autonomous, less intervention

Sentinel as designed is deliberately conservative. TALON proposes, Asha authorizes, every time. That's the right call for a new system with early models. But it's not the end state.

As TALON's models improve on real incident data — and as the override patterns reveal which decisions Asha consistently approves without modification — the authority boundary shifts. The future version looks like TALON handling containment and evacuation routing autonomously, with Asha only brought in for genuinely novel situations the system can't resolve. The operator role moves from authorizing routine escalations to arbitrating real edge cases.

The trajectory: Today — operator authorizes every escalation. Near-term — operator handles major decisions, AI handles sub-routing. Far-term — AI handles the full response pipeline, trained on terrain-specific and incident-specific data, with the operator as a true exception handler. The audit trail Sentinel generates now is the training signal that makes that delegation safe later. We're collecting it now to delegate responsibly later.
