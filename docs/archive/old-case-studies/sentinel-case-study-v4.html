TL;DR — For the scanners

The bottleneck in wildfire response is cognitive, not technological. Sentinel redesigns the interface as a decision surface — the AI handles triage and execution, the human owns the three moments where judgment actually matters.

Every decision in this project is an answer to the same question: what does the operator need to do right now, and what can the system safely carry for them?

01 — The Problem

48 minutes. And it's not a drone problem.

California already has the hardware. It doesn't have an interface worthy of it.

Sensor networks catch thermal anomalies in seconds. Drones can be airborne in under a minute. So why does the average detection-to-deployment window still sit at 48 minutes? Every solution in this space attacks the hardware — faster drones, more sensors, wider satellite coverage. None of it moves the number. Because the number isn't a hardware problem.

Here's what's actually happening when that timer runs. The operator gets a thermal alert alongside three others from different zones, a satellite feed on a second monitor, and a ground sensor log no one's touched since the last shift. They mentally try to stitch it all together. Is this a controlled burn? What's the wind doing? How far is the nearest housing cluster? Who's on-call at Station 12? None of those questions require human judgment — the data to answer them exists. But no interface assembles it. So the operator does, at 4 AM, under time pressure, six hours into a shift.

That assembly process is where the 48 minutes go.

The real problem statement: There's no interface that handles cognitive assembly between detection and deployment. Operators are doing machine-speed data aggregation with human-speed brains — under conditions specifically designed to produce errors.

I also want to be upfront about what I intentionally left out. No manual drone joystick controls. No mobile app. Operators should be authorizing outcomes, not piloting individual assets. Manual flight controls would recreate the exact bottleneck I was trying to eliminate. And a mobile app is for field teams — you can't reason spatially about a spreading wildfire on a 6-inch screen. Sometimes the most important design decision is what you say no to.

02 — The Messy Middle

My first prototype tried to automate the crisis. It accidentally cut the human out entirely.

Early on, I was fixated on a research finding: operators under extreme stress experience cognitive tunneling — they fixate on one data point and lose everything else. The obvious design response felt elegant: compress the decision tree as much as possible. Remove the complexity. Make it impossible to get lost.

The Escalation Slider — what it was

I designed a horizontal control at the bottom of the screen. Drag a handle from Assess → Contain → Rescue. Each position triggers a predefined AI macro-script — a set of drone deployments, zone classifications, and notification sequences that execute automatically when the slider hits that stop. Clean. Fast. Confident-looking.

The core problem: a slider implies a script. When Asha drags to Contain, she's blindly trusting an execution she never reviewed — no geometry, no drone positions, no idea which residential structures fall inside the projected spread zone. A slider doesn't record a conscious decision. It records a gesture. And when 47 households get evacuated based on that gesture, the audit trail just says "operator moved slider at 03:48." That's not authorization. That's abdication.

The wireframes had another issue too. I'd built a dark-mode dashboard — drone telemetry panels, altitude readouts, sensor graphs everywhere. Roughly 120 text elements. I was solving cognitive load while actively creating it.

03 — Design Decisions

Six pivots. Each one moved where the line sits.

These aren't aesthetic refinements. Each one is a change in the authority boundary — where TALON's responsibility ends and Asha's begins.

1
Forced navigation → interrupt design
Alert snapping to a new screen destroyed the operator's spatial context every single time.
Visual reference · Pivot 01
[ Insert: side-by-side — full screen context switch vs. modal overlay on live map ]
Before
Anomaly detected → entire UI jumps to an Alert screen. Everything the operator was watching is gone.
After
Alert arrives as a floating modal over the live map. Spatial context stays intact. Asha responds without disorientation.

Operators don't have "alert time" and "non-alert time." They're always mid-task. This also crystallised a distinction I'd been blurring: an interrupt demands a decision on top of current state — a transition means the operator commits to a new phase entirely. Once I separated those two things, every subsequent navigation decision got easier.

2
Replay verification → live signal classification
A reassuring clip of the incident felt trustworthy. It was also factually impossible — fire doesn't have a replay.
Visual reference · Pivot 02
[ Insert: alert modal showing classification inputs — prior burns, wind vector, thermal delta above baseline ]
Before
A short "verification replay" to build confidence. Borrowed from consumer product thinking. Didn't reflect how the system actually works.
After
TALON classification: ignition probability with the contributing factors surfaced openly — prior burn history, wind vector, thermal delta above baseline.

The deeper failure: I was designing trust through familiarity rather than accuracy. A score alone gives Asha nothing to push back against. Showing her the reasoning — the actual inputs that produced the conclusion — gives her the basis to agree with TALON, or to say it's wrong. That's a real decision. A confidence bar is not.

3
Uniform density → progressive information scaling
Same ~120 text elements across every phase. Reading paragraphs of AI rationale during an active rescue is a design failure.
📉
Diagram · 03
Progressive density — 4 panel states
Replace with: 4 panel screenshots side-by-side showing density drop from Scan (~120 elements) through Verify, Contain, to Rescue (~25 elements)
Before
Same density everywhere. Full paragraphs of TALON's reasoning visible during active evacuation.
After
Scan: verbose context. Contain: headlines + status dots. Rescue: zero prose, ~25 elements, large numbers, one log entry.

Under stress, people read numbers and spatial cues measurably faster than prose. The interface has to breathe with Asha's cognitive load. Every panel component needed three distinct render states — the Intel panel in Scan and the Intel panel in Rescue occupy the same screen position, but they have completely different contracts with the operator.

4
Action toolbox → consequence-tiered action bar
"Notify Authorities" sitting next to "Emergency Evacuate" in the same row. Proximity creates accidental authority.
Visual reference · Pivot 04
[ Insert: before/after action bar — flat row of all actions vs. tiered primary/secondary with chevron tray ]
Before
All actions in one scrolling row. Administrative tasks — the stuff that can wait — at the same visual weight as life-safety decisions.
After
Saving Lives > Staging Hardware > Filing Reports. Primary actions front and center. Secondary behind a chevron tray. In Rescue: no primary CTA at all.

The Rescue screen having no dominant button was a deliberate choice, and it's one I had to fight for internally. The instinct is always to give the operator something to do. But when the system is executing correctly, Asha's job is to supervise — not initiate. The absence of a button is the signal that everything is working.

5
Free-text evacuation form → pre-staged team chips
The operator typing "Route A, 15 personnel" during peak stress. Wrong interaction model entirely.
Visual reference · Pivot 05
[ Insert: old free-text modal vs. new team chip selection with zone assignment ]
Before
Evacuation modal with HTML text inputs. Type the route. Type a headcount. "15 personnel" isn't how ground teams actually work.
After
Role-based team chips: Alpha Lead, Bravo, Charlie. Assign team to zone. System filters valid zones by team certification. Assignments made in Contain — not decided under Rescue pressure for the first time.

Typing during a stress spike forces translation from spatial reality into words. But the more important change is the pre-staging: by the time Asha reaches Rescue, the team assignments are already locked in from Contain. The evacuation confirmation becomes a review of a plan she already approved — not a cold first decision at the worst possible moment.

6
Anonymous AI → TALON, accountable system agent
"AI Confidence 84%" is a black box. You can't audit "AI." You can audit TALON.
Visual reference · Pivot 06
[ Insert: activity log comparison — "AI: action" vs. "TALON · 03:47:18 UTC: corroboration threshold crossed" ]
Before
"AI Confidence," "AI Map," "AI Recommends." Generic labels. No attribution, no audit trail, no way to trace a decision back to its source.
After
TALON callsign. Every AI action logs separately from operator actions, in cyan. Aerospace nomenclature: Anomaly Validation, Telemetry Log, TALON Spatial Reconstruction.

This isn't branding. It's an accountability mechanism. When Asha is managing 47 households at 4 AM, she needs to know which decisions were hers and which were the system's — and so does the incident review six weeks later. If it sounds like a consumer chatbot, she won't trust it with human lives. And she shouldn't.

I realised pretty early on that operators don't actually trust black-box percentages — they trust behaviour. So I built TALON's UI on four principles that earn trust under fire, rather than just claiming it:

Transparency
TALON shows why it made a choice — satellite source, thermal confidence, wind vector — not just the conclusion. The reasoning is the interface.
Consistency
The layout never jumps. Panels stay in place. Actions appear in the same location every phase. Under stress the brain relies on spatial memory — this UI cannot violate it.
Accountability
TALON signs every autonomous action in the audit log. Human and machine decisions are always distinguishable, always reviewable, permanently on record.
Reversibility
Every AI proposal has a one-tap Override. No TALON action is irreversible without explicit authorization. If it can't be undone, it can't be trusted.

Trust isn't a feature. It's the cumulative effect of every interaction behaving the way the operator expects — especially when the fire isn't.

The map is Reality — 3D. Photorealistic terrain, fire volume, wind-constrained topography. Visceral and unpredictable, because it is. The UI is Intelligence — 2D. Drone positions, zones, spread estimates as scalable vectors floating above. Clean, crisp, instantly legible. Neither layer competes for the same visual depth plane — the operator can tell what is happening versus what the system is proposing to do about it, even half-asleep at 4 AM.

This split also directly handles the smoke degradation failure: because the Intelligence layer is independent of the camera feed, TALON falls back to LiDAR when smoke kills the optics — and Asha loses video without losing her spatial picture.

Visual Ergonomics · A failure from testing

I used proportional fonts for all countdown timers in the early prototype. Looked refined in Figma. In live testing, the digits jumped horizontally every second as narrow and wide characters swapped in and out. Invisible on a static screen. Six hours into a night shift it causes real eye strain.

The fix: Switched all time-critical displays to font-variant-numeric: tabular-nums. Every digit takes identical horizontal space. The baseline never moves. A crisis UI cannot have moving baselines — and you'll never catch it unless the system is actually running.
06 — Designing for Chaos

When the happy path dies.

In aerospace, the "happy path" is a myth. A system designed only for perfect conditions is a liability.

A wildfire at 4 AM is not a controlled environment. Smoke severs drone feeds. The AI gets contradicting signals. Dozens of minor faults pile up at once. Each of these is a designed state — not an unhandled error. I designed for the chaos scenarios last, but they're actually where the most interesting product thinking happened. The way a system fails is the most honest signal of its design philosophy.

📡
Chaos 01 · Hardware
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
What happens
Dense smoke and mountain terrain sever camera feeds at the exact moments the operator needs them most. Most systems respond with a "Connection Lost" modal and a gap in the map. The operator is flying blind at peak urgency, with a UI that's stopped being useful.
TALON's fallback
When a drone loses optical feed, the UI doesn't throw a blocking error. TALON falls back automatically to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction. Asha loses live video — she keeps spatial awareness, drone position, and telemetry. The degraded state surfaces as a single status chip in the left panel. Not a modal, not an interruption. She can acknowledge it when cognitive load permits. The mission continues. This fallback is also why the 3D base layer exists in the first place — when the camera can't see through smoke, the terrain model has to carry the spatial picture alone.
⚠️
Chaos 02 · AI Contradiction
Contradicting signals — when TALON says "I don't know"
What happens
The satellite feed reads "Active fire signature" at Grid 4C. The ground sensor at the same location reads "Clear." Two authoritative sources, directly contradicting each other. AI is usually presented as an oracle. This is the scenario that exposes the lie.
Designed response
TALON doesn't synthesize a false confidence score or pick a side. The UI shows both conflicting signals side by side. The recommendation field is left intentionally blank — TALON explicitly says nothing, because it doesn't have enough to say. The priority card reads: "Conflicting source data — operator judgment required." Asha breaks the tie. She has context TALON doesn't: whether that ground sensor has been unreliable lately, whether there was maintenance near that grid, whether the satellite pass is hours old. An AI that forces a recommendation when it genuinely doesn't know is more dangerous than one that admits uncertainty.
🌪️
Chaos 03 · Real-Time Data Shift
The mid-decision confidence flip
What happens
Asha is hovering over AUTHORIZE CONTAINMENT. Confidence is at 88%. Her hand is essentially on the decision. Wind shifts. A new thermal reading comes in. TALON's confidence drops to 41% in three seconds. This is the most dangerous moment in the entire flow — a decision already in motion, interrupted by data that invalidates it.
Designed response
The UI does not throw a blocking error modal. A sudden panic modal at the moment of authorization causes Asha to make a worse decision, not a better one. Instead: the AUTHORIZE button instantly downgrades from amber to disabled grey. The priority card pulses a non-blocking warning: "Data Shift: Review Required." The map updates with the new wind vector and revised spread projection. Asha sees the changed picture. She can reassess and re-authorize when she's ready — or modify the plan. The system communicates urgency without manufacturing panic.
💥
Chaos 04 · Compound Failure
Everything breaks at once — confidence drops, drone fails, second alert arrives
What happens
At 03:49:12 — 21 seconds into Rescue — TALON's confidence drops from 84% to 39% due to a wind shift. Simultaneously, Scout-02 loses its feed (smoke cover). While Asha is processing both, a second thermal alert fires in Grid 6A, six kilometres north. Three things going wrong at the same time, in the phase specifically designed to have no primary action button.
Designed response
This is the scenario I thought hardest about. The temptation is to throw everything at the operator simultaneously — three alerts, three modals, maximum urgency. That's the design that gets people killed. Instead: the Exception Feed stacks the events ranked by consequence severity, not arrival time. Scout-02's degradation becomes a LiDAR fallback silently — it surfaces as a chip, not a modal. The confidence drop downgrades the Rescue action bar state visually. The new Grid 6A alert arrives as a second interrupt, but it cannot stack over the active Rescue phase — it queues. Asha handles the active crisis. The new incident waits in the priority feed with its own TALON pre-assessment ready for when she's free. The system serialises the chaos so the operator doesn't have to.
Design decision · What happens when the operator dismisses an alert and the fire spreads anyway
Rejected
The System Override. If Asha dismisses an alert and the fire spreads anyway, the system secretly re-activates the containment protocol without telling her.
Chosen
Re-Escalation as a New Event. The spread is treated as a brand-new incident — interrupting Asha with a "Critical Update" alert and forcing a fresh, conscious decision.
Why
If the system can secretly override Asha's call, then the system is the Captain and Asha is the illusion of control. The system can challenge her with new data. It cannot steal her authority.
Last resort: ABORT MISSION
Every scenario above has a designed response. But some failures exceed the system's scope entirely — cascading hardware failure, total comms blackout, a situation TALON has no model for. ABORT MISSION is always in the action bar. Always one tap. Always the same position. No confirmation modal. When something is catastrophically wrong, Asha should not be hunting through menus to make it stop.
07 — Reflection

The honest version.

The 90-second claim is a hypothesis, not a proof.

I want to be straight about this. The binding constraint in wildfire response might not be the interface at all — it might be radio protocol between dispatch and field units, or network latency in smoke-heavy terrain, or the fact that TALON's classification model was trained on historical data that doesn't yet account for how fire behaves in a changing climate. I designed for the interface variable without validating whether it's actually the bottleneck. That's a real gap.

What I'd do differently

The override flow is the hardest part of this system and the part I'd go back and properly stress-test. It's where the human-AI authority boundary is most contested — the moment when Asha knows something TALON doesn't, and the system has to hand control back gracefully without aborting what's already in motion. I designed the clean version of that interaction. I didn't put it in front of real dispatchers. That's where Sentinel is most likely to break, and it's exactly where I'd spend the next iteration.

What I'd actually measure: Time from alert interrupt to operator decision. Decision accuracy — how often dismissed alerts became real incidents. Operator override rate — too low might mean Asha trusts TALON more than she should. Exception acknowledgment lag — how long non-critical faults sit unread before compounding.

What comes after

Sentinel was designed for single-incident command. The real next problem is multi-incident coordination — simultaneous fires across sectors, parallel command contexts, operator handoffs mid-crisis. The underlying architecture could support it; the interface doesn't yet. And honestly? The version I'd really love to design is the full state-of-emergency mode. Multiple simultaneous crises, coordinating across agencies, everything happening at once. Basically zombie-apocalypse-level coordination. That's the problem that actually excites me next.

"I started designing screens. I ended up designing a decision-making system under time pressure. Those are not the same thing — and every pivot in this case study is the exact moment I noticed the difference."

The interface disappears during action. What remains is exactly the decision the operator needs to make — nothing more.
