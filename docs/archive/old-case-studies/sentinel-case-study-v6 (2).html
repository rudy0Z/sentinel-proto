FlytBase Sentinel — Design Fusion S3 Runner-up — 2025

Autonomous wildfire response, designed for the operator — not the drone.

Wildfire operators managing drone fleets were spending 48 minutes manually cross-referencing sensor data, satellite imagery, and permit records before they could authorize any response. The hardware could deploy in under 30 seconds. Sentinel is the AI interface that closes that gap — compressing the decision window to 90 seconds while keeping the operator in explicit control of every call that matters.

01The problem

Operators were the bottleneck — not the hardware.

When a sensor detects a potential fire, deploying drones over populated areas requires verification first: checking thermal readings, cross-referencing satellite data, and confirming burn permits across three separate systems — all manually, under time pressure. By the time an operator had assembled enough context to act, an average of 48 minutes had passed.

48 min
Average time from initial alert to first authorized action — spent manually assembling context across disconnected systems.
90 sec
Target time to verify the incident and send an authority notification. Staging and containment follow after.
3
Explicit operator decisions in the entire verification window. Everything else is handled by the AI — with a full audit trail.
Root cause

The three systems operators relied on — thermal sensors, satellite feeds, and municipal burn permits — had no shared interface. Operators were manually translating between them under pressure. That translation work, not hardware capability, was the 48 minutes.

Validation note

The 90-second target is based on simulation walkthroughs against documented emergency dispatch protocols — not field-measured data from real operators. This gap is addressed honestly in the outcomes section.

02System structure

Two operating modes, separated by a single checkpoint.

The interface runs in two modes. The first 90 seconds focus entirely on verifying whether the incident is real and notifying civil authorities. Everything after — drone staging, evacuation, ground team management — happens in the second mode once that notification is sent. The interface automatically adjusts what it shows based on which mode is active.

Phase A · Intelligence · 0–90 sec
Is this a real fire? Should authorities be notified?

The operator verifies the anomaly, reviews evidence, resolves any data conflicts, and sends the authority notification. The interface shows everything relevant — full sensor context, AI reasoning, and evidence sources.

Phase B · Operations · Post-notification
Coordinate resources, evacuate residents, supervise containment.

Drones executing, evacuation active, biometrics live. The interface automatically strips to compact single-line telemetry — no prose, exceptions only. The operator supervises rather than directs.

03The verification flow

From thermal alert to authorized notification in 90 seconds.

The operator — a sector lead named Asha managing multiple autonomous drone loops — receives four phase transitions during the verification window. TALON works silently between them. Each transition fires only when there's a real decision to make.

Scan0 – 15 sec

An alert fires. TALON has already assembled a brief from available sensor data. Asha reads it and makes one decision: dispatch a verification drone, or dismiss.

System

Sensor Fusion assembles the incident brief before the alert fires — thermal readings, prior incident history, current burn permits. Asha gets a complete picture rather than raw inputs.

Operator

Reviews the brief without leaving the map view. The primary action is Dispatch Scout-02. No context switching between systems.

Verify15 – 45 sec

Scout en route. TALON fuses incoming sensor data in the background. The interface stays quiet — no updates unless something genuinely changes.

TALON only interrupts when evidence crosses a meaningful threshold
System

Fire Behavior and Values at Risk agents run silently. TALON fires the next interrupt only when evidence crosses a threshold — three independent sensor confirmations minimum. If sensor and permit data conflict, the Forced Calibrator gate fires (FS-01).

Operator

Continues monitoring other zones. She's not watching a progress bar — TALON will interrupt when there's something to decide. With no conflict, the next interrupt arrives with fully converged evidence.

Evidence has converged. The notification packet is ready. Asha reviews each source individually and authorizes the notification to civil authorities.

Displays an evidence matrix — each contributing sensor as a labeled chip with its own confirmation status. Structures at risk shown prominently. The packet is assembled and waiting, but nothing sends until Asha approves it.

Reviews the individual sources — she can see exactly which data is confirmed and which is pending. Clicks "Notify Authorities." The 90-second timer stops. The interface shifts to Phase B.

04Design decisions

Five decisions with meaningful trade-offs.

These were the choices that shaped how the system behaves — each one required giving something up to get the more important thing right.

D—01

Limiting the 90 seconds to verification, not full containment

Core scope

The original prototype claimed full wildfire containment could be authorized in 90 seconds — including drone staging, evacuation, and residential clearance. That's not operationally possible before an incident is even verified.

A
Keep the full-containment claim
Dropped — real emergency protocols require verification before any tactical deployment. The claim collapses the moment someone with field experience looks at it.
B
Remove the human gate, let the AI authorize automatically
Dropped — autonomous deployment over residential areas requires explicit operator authorization. A regulatory constraint, not a design preference.
C
90 seconds ends at authority notification — containment follows in Phase B
The packet is sent with Asha's explicit sign-off. Staging and containment proceed after, without an artificial time constraint.
Trade-off

The original metric sounds more impressive. The chosen framing is what actually holds up in a real emergency dispatch conversation.

D—02

Requiring explicit confirmation when sensor and permit data conflict

AI governance

Under time pressure, operators tend to accept AI recommendations without scrutinizing them — a known pattern in emergency dispatch called automation bias. In wildfire response, this leads to false-positive deployments over residential areas.

A
Let TALON silently resolve permit conflicts in the background
Dropped — sensor and satellite data don't update at the same rate. A silent resolution can commit to the wrong conclusion before the conflict has actually resolved.
B
Block the interface with a modal until satellite data catches up
Dropped — covering the map canvas during an active size-up removes the spatial context Asha needs. A modal here solves the wrong problem.
C
Surface the conflict, lock primary actions, require a manual verification checkbox
The Forced Calibrator. Asha must confirm she's checked the permit database before any action re-enables. The map stays fully visible throughout.
Trade-off

Adds 4–6 seconds of mandatory friction on every permit conflict. That friction is proportional to what it prevents: a deployment over occupied housing based on incomplete information.

D—03

Showing individual sensor readings rather than a single confidence score

AI transparency

TALON originally surfaced a single number: "87% ignition probability." Operators could accept or reject the recommendation, but had nothing concrete to scrutinize or push back on.

A
Single confidence score
Dropped — a number that can't be interrogated reduces the operator to someone who can only accept or reject. It doesn't support a meaningful override.
B
Confidence score with a paragraph explanation
Dropped — reading prose doesn't fit a 90-second decision window. The format doesn't support rapid scanning under pressure.
C
Evidence matrix — one chip per source, each showing its own state
Thermal delta, LiDAR, satellite IR, burn permit — each labeled so Asha sees exactly which input is confirmed, pending, or conflicting.
Trade-off

Takes more screen space than a single number. Worth it because operators who understand which signal drove a recommendation make better overrides — and correct overrides improve the system over time.

D—04

Switching to vector coordinates when cell bandwidth becomes unusable

Infrastructure

Remote wildfire perimeters run on degraded cell connectivity, not fiber. Above 300ms latency, HD video feeds saturate the available bandwidth. Standard interfaces freeze behind loading indicators — useless during an active incident.

A
Show loading indicators and wait for the feed to buffer
Dropped — a frozen interface during an active response is worse than a degraded one. Spatial awareness gaps during rescue carry direct operational cost.
B
Fall back to audio-only radio coordination
Dropped — eliminates all spatial awareness of drone positions, evacuation routes, and zone boundaries. Not viable for a command interface.
C
Drop HD video, fade satellite imagery, prioritize lightweight LiDAR vectors
Above 300ms, the interface switches modes automatically — satellite opacity drops to near-zero, drone positions and paths stay fully live as vector overlays.
Trade-off

Asha loses the camera feeds. A responsive interface with accurate coordinates is more useful than a high-fidelity one arriving 30 seconds late.

D—05

Automatically reducing interface density as the incident escalates

Cognitive load

The same amount of information was displayed across all phases — including during peak Operations, when the operator's attention is most divided across active coordination tasks.

A
Separate layout per phase
Dropped — forces Asha to reorient her spatial model at a moment when cognitive overhead is already high.
B
Manual density toggle — let the operator choose
Dropped — adds a preference decision at the worst possible moment. Operators shouldn't be configuring their interface mid-incident.
C
Automatic density scaling — same grid, less shown as urgency rises
Detailed drone cards in the Intelligence phase; compact single-line strips in Operations. The spatial model stays consistent — only information depth changes.
Trade-off

The operator has less control over their information view. Justified because reducing available decisions during peak load is a feature, not a limitation.

06What I changed

Two early directions that didn't work.

V0.1 · discardedSlider escalation modelscreens/iter-slider

A slider for moving between response stages

Moving a slider felt like adjusting a setting, not authorizing a deployment. In testing, operators described it as a volume knob — they were moving the UI forward, not making a conscious decision. The gesture eliminated the moment of approval entirely.

V1 scope · correctedOriginal 90-second scopescreens/iter-scope

Claiming full containment could be authorized in 90 seconds

Staging containment lines requires knowing the fire's size and direction. Evacuating residents requires knowing which areas are at risk. Neither is possible before verification. Correcting this changed the scope of the entire design — the 90 seconds became a verification-only window.

08Results & reflection

What the design achieved, and what I'd do differently.

Primary validation
Time to first decision

From alert to dispatch action. Target under 15 seconds. If consistently above that, the brief format isn't surfacing the right information first — a design problem, not a training problem.

Safety signal
Dismissed alert accuracy

What percentage of dismissed alerts turned out to be real incidents? Anything above 2% requires immediate review. The goal is accurate dismissals, not zero.

Trust calibration
Operator override rate

Target 15–40%. Too low means operators are rubber-stamping. Too high means TALON's plans aren't reliable enough to act on. Both are failure modes.

Governance check
Calibrator false-positive rate

How often does the Forced Calibrator fire on a genuine fire, not a controlled burn? Friction should be proportional to actual conflict probability.

What still needs real validation

The 90-second target comes from simulation walkthroughs, not field-testing with trained operators. Whether the Forced Calibrator's friction is acceptable under actual dispatch pressure is something I couldn't determine without real users. TALON's agent latency under genuine network degradation is also untested.

What I'd change if I started again

Less time on the Operations density transitions, more time testing the evidence matrix with actual operators. The question I never answered: does the chip-based layout match how dispatchers reason about verification, or does their mental model work differently? I assumed it would — I don't have data to confirm it.

If the system can make a call without Asha knowing, Asha isn't in control — she just thinks she is.
