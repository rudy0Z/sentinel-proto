It's 4 AM. A thermal spike fires in the steep foothills of Altadena.
Asha Rao has 90 seconds.

The nearest residential grid is 800 metres from the forest edge. Under current wind conditions — 18 mph, northeast — fire can reach it in under 30 minutes. The standard dispatch process involves manually cross-referencing three separate systems, calling two department liaisons, and filling out a pre-deployment form. That process takes 48 minutes on a good night. Tonight is not a good night.

Sentinel was built to collapse that window. Not by removing humans from the loop — but by redesigning exactly where in the loop they belong.

TL;DR — For the scanners

The bottleneck in wildfire response is cognitive, not technological. Sentinel redesigns the operator interface as a decision surface — AI handles triage and execution, humans own the three moments where judgment is irreplaceable.

Every design decision in this project answers the same question: what does the operator actually need to do right now — and what can the system safely own for them?

01 — Problem

48 minutes. Not a hardware problem.

California has the drones. It doesn't have an interface worthy of them.

Sensor networks detect thermal anomalies within seconds. Drones can be airborne in under a minute. But the average detection-to-deployment window in wildfire response is still 48 minutes — because the operator sits between hardware that's fast and a system that isn't designed to help them think fast.

Operators are receiving thermal alerts, satellite feeds, wind vectors, and ground sensor data from disconnected systems. Under time pressure at 4 AM, they manually assemble a threat picture in their head, make a deployment call, then translate that call into a sequence of forms and radio communications. That translation layer is where the 48 minutes go.

The reframe: The problem isn't sensor coverage or drone range. It's that no interface currently handles the cognitive assembly work — leaving operators to do it under conditions specifically designed to cause errors: sleep deprivation, time pressure, and fragmented information sources.

Intentional omission What we explicitly didn't build
No manual drone joystick controls. No mobile app. Operators authorize outcomes, not actions. Manual flight controls invert the human-AI relationship and create the exact micromanagement bottleneck the system was built to eliminate. Mobile apps are for field teams; command centers require the screen real estate to reason spatially about a wildfire spreading across a 3D terrain. Saying no to these wasn't a scope decision — it was a product philosophy decision.

03 — Information Architecture

The operator's mental mode, mapped to what the system shows them.

Sentinel is a state machine. The entire UI locks to one phase at a time — phase determines information density, panel content, available actions, and color register. The IA isn't organized around what the system knows. It's organized around what Asha needs to do next.

Design decision · Accountability TALON: from anonymous AI to auditable system agent
Generic labels — "AI Confidence," "AI Recommends" — are black boxes. Operators in life-critical environments are appropriately skeptical of systems that claim authority without traceable accountability. We renamed the intelligence engine TALON. Every autonomous action logs under the TALON callsign with a timestamp. TALON entries render in cyan in the Activity Log, separated from operator entries. This creates a complete audit trail where human and machine actions are always distinguishable — and individually reviewable. TALON can be audited. "AI" cannot.

Design decision · Visual architecture Reality layer vs. Intelligence layer
Two traps in command center map design: a flat lifeless map lacking threat context, or 3D drones on 3D terrain that looks like a mobile strategy game. Sentinel separates these into two explicit layers. The map is Reality — photorealistic 3D topography, fire volume, wind-constrained terrain. The UI is Intelligence — drone positions, zone boundaries, spread estimates as sharp infinitely-scalable 2D vectors floating above. This creates "Visual Silence": the operator instantly distinguishes unpredictable physical environment from structured system assets — without either competing for the same visual depth plane.
04 — Design Decisions

Six pivots. Each one changed the authority model.

These aren't visual refinements — each one represents a change in where the boundary sits between TALON's authority and Asha's judgment.

1
Forced navigation → interrupt design
Alert = context switch  →  Alert = overlay on live state
Before
Anomaly detected → entire UI snaps to an Alert screen. Operator's spatial context destroyed on every alert.
After
Alert arrives as floating modal overlay on the live map. Operator's spatial context stays intact. They respond without losing orientation.

Operators don't have "alert time" and "non-alert time." They're always mid-task. The interrupt model clarified a distinction we'd been conflating: an interrupt demands a decision on top of current state. A transition means the operator commits to a new phase. Once that was clear, every subsequent navigation decision became easier to make.

2
Replay verification → live signal classification
Trust through familiarity  →  Trust through accuracy
Before
A "verification replay" clip — show the operator a short video to build confidence. Familiar. Reassuring. Factually wrong.
After
TALON classification: ignition probability from historical burn data for this grid. The reasoning inputs are surfaced alongside the score — prior burns, wind vector, thermal delta above baseline.

Fire doesn't have a replay. Detection comes from thermal sensors and satellite signals. We were borrowing a consumer product mental model. The deeper problem: we were designing trust through familiarity rather than accuracy. Showing the reasoning gives Asha the basis to agree or push back. A percentage alone gives her nothing to work with.

3
Uniform density → progressive information scaling
~120 elements across all phases  →  density scales inversely with urgency
Before
Same text density across all phases. ~120 readable elements in Contain. Paragraphs of AI rationale during maximum urgency.
After
Scan = verbose. Contain = headlines + status dots. Rescue = zero prose, ~25 elements, large numbers, 1 log entry.

Under stress, humans process numbers and spatial cues faster than prose. The interface must breathe with the operator's cognitive load. Every panel component needed three render states. The Intel panel in Scan is a different component from the Intel panel in Rescue — same position, different contract.

4
Action toolbox → consequence-tiered action bar
All actions visible  →  primary CTA by phase, secondary behind tray
Before
"Notify Authorities" next to "Emergency Evacuate" in the same row. Administrative tasks sharing visual weight with life-safety decisions.
After
Saving Lives > Staging Hardware > Filing Reports. Primary forward-actions visible. Administrative/secondary behind a chevron tray. In Rescue, no primary CTA at all — its absence is the signal.

Proximity creates accidental authority. In a crisis, the cognitive cost of avoiding a destructive action you can see is always present. The bar communicates system state through what's on it and what isn't.

5
Free-text evacuation form → pre-staged role-based chips
Type "Route A, 15 personnel"  →  tap team chip, assign to zone
Before
Evacuation modal with free-text inputs. Operator types route name, types a headcount. "15 personnel" is not how ground teams work.
After
Role-based team chips (Alpha Lead, Bravo, Charlie). Tap to assign team to zone. System filters valid zones by team certification. Assignments made in Contain — evacuation modal is a review, not a first decision.

Typing during a stress spike forces translation from spatial reality to words. The bigger change is pre-staging: by the time Asha reaches Rescue, team assignments are already authorized. The confirmation is reviewing a staged plan — not deciding under maximum pressure for the first time.

6
Anonymous AI → TALON, accountable system agent
"AI Confidence 84%"  →  TALON: classified high ignition risk · 03:47 UTC
Before
"AI Confidence," "AI Map," "AI Recommends." Generic black-box labels with no attribution or audit trail.
After
TALON callsign. Every AI action logs separately from operator actions. Cyan in the Activity Log. Aerospace nomenclature: Anomaly Validation, Telemetry Log, TALON Spatial Reconstruction.

This isn't branding — it's an accountability mechanism. TALON can be audited. "AI" cannot. The language shift extended across the full interface: if it sounds like a consumer chatbot, Asha won't trust it with 47 households at 4 AM.

06 — Edge Cases

What happens when the system doesn't work cleanly.

A wildfire at 4 AM is not a controlled environment. Thick smoke severs drone feeds. AI models get it wrong. Dozens of minor faults surface simultaneously. Each of these is a designed state — not an unhandled error.

📡
Edge Case 01
Signal degradation — the "blind" drone
The reality
Dense smoke cover and mountainous terrain frequently sever visual camera feeds in active wildfire zones. This happens in exactly the moments the operator most needs the drone.
TALON response
When a drone loses optical feed, the UI does not throw a "Connection Lost" error and leave a gap in the map. TALON automatically falls back to LiDAR + terrain model reconstruction. The operator loses live video — but retains spatial awareness, telemetry, and drone position. The mission continues. The degraded state surfaces in the panel as a single status chip, not a blocking modal.
⚡
Edge Case 02
AI classification error — operator override
The reality
TALON's thermal corroboration model will sometimes be wrong. Or Asha will spot a geographic variable the model missed — a dry creek bed the AI doesn't know is a fire corridor, a road that's blocked.
Override flow
TALON is the First Officer. Asha is the Captain. If the proposed containment perimeter is wrong, she taps Override and the UI shifts from Authorization Mode back to Manual Staging Mode — transferring full geographic control back to the human. Underlying drone deployments already underway are not aborted. The plan modifies around what's already in motion. Every override logs under Asha's operator ID, not TALON's.
🔔
Edge Case 03
Exception management — preventing alert fatigue
The reality
With 6 drones airborne in a fire zone, there will be dozens of minor faults: battery dips, slight route deviations, temporary signal losses. If every fault throws a red modal, Asha stops seeing the critical ones.
Exception feed
Non-critical failures route silently to the Exception Feed in the right panel — never to a blocking modal. Asha can batch-acknowledge them when cognitive load permits. Only genuinely decision-requiring exceptions interrupt the primary visual space. The design problem isn't building a good alert — it's building a good filter.
Last resort: ABORT MISSION
Every scenario above has a designed response. But for failures that exceed system scope — cascading drone hardware failure, total comms blackout, a scenario TALON has no model for — ABORT MISSION lives in the action bar as a persistent secondary action. It is always one tap away, always in the same position. It does not require a confirmation modal. When something is catastrophically wrong, the operator should not be hunting through menus.
07 — Reflection

The honest version of what I built — and what it can't do yet.

The 90-second window is a testable hypothesis. The actual binding constraint may not be the interface at all — it may be radio protocol between dispatch and field units, or network latency in smoke-heavy terrain where drone feeds degrade. I designed for the interface variable without validating it against the other constraints.

TALON's classification model surfaces historical ignition probability. But terrain reconstruction accuracy degrades under active fire conditions — smoke, thermal noise, and rapid perimeter change will reduce model confidence in exactly the moments it matters most. The design surfaces degradation as a chip. Whether that's sufficient information for a judgment call is an open question.

What I'd measure, if shipped: Time from alert interrupt to operator decision (dispatch / dismiss). Decision accuracy — dismissed alerts that became incidents. Operator override rate — too low may mean Asha trusts TALON more than she should. Exception acknowledgment delay — how long non-critical faults sit unacknowledged before they compound.

"I started designing screens. I ended up designing a decision-making system. The two things are not the same, and every pivot in this case study is the specific moment I noticed the difference."

The interface disappears during action. What remains is exactly the decision the operator needs to make — nothing more.
