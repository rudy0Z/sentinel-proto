01The problem

The bottleneck was never the hardware. It was the operator's interface.

FlytBase drones can be airborne and streaming thermal video within 30 seconds of a detection. So why does it take 48 minutes to respond? Because before anyone can put a drone over a neighbourhood, the incident has to be verified — and verification lived across three systems that didn't talk to each other.

An operator flagged a thermal reading on one screen, cross-checked satellite imagery on another, and confirmed there was no registered controlled burn in a third — all manually, at 3 AM, under pressure. That translation work between disconnected systems was the 48 minutes. The fire kept moving the entire time.

48 min

Average time from first alert to authorized action — spent assembling context across systems that don't share an interface.

90 sec

Target to verify the incident and send an authority notification. Containment and rescue planning follow — no artificial timer.

3

Explicit operator decisions inside the verification window. Everything else the AI handles, with a full audit trail.

Root cause

Thermal sensors, satellite feeds, and municipal burn permits had no shared surface. The operator was the integration layer — translating between three sources under time pressure. That manual translation, not drone speed, was the binding constraint. So I designed for the operator's cognition, not the fleet's capability.

02The approach

Verify first, operate second — split by a single human checkpoint.

The interface runs in two modes. The first 90 seconds are about one question only: is this real, and should authorities be told? Everything else — staging drones, evacuating residents, coordinating ground teams — happens after that notification is sent.

Phase A · Intelligence · 0–90 sec
Is this a real fire? Do authorities need to know now?

The operator verifies the anomaly, reviews each evidence source, resolves any conflicts, and sends the authority packet. Full sensor context, AI reasoning, and every source on screen.

Phase B · Operations · After notification
Coordinate resources, evacuate, supervise containment.

Drones executing, evacuation live, biometrics streaming. The interface strips down to single-line telemetry and exceptions only. The operator supervises rather than directs.

That split is what makes the 90-second claim survivable in a conversation with a real incident commander — and it's the single biggest change from where this project started. The AI carries the load inside it.

04Design decisions

Where the real calls were made.

I needed to define where the human oversight lived, how the AI accounted for itself, and when the system had to remain silent. These were the pivots.

D—01

Forcing a manual check when sensor and permit data disagree

AI governance

Under time pressure, operators rubber-stamp AI to relieve cognitive load — automation bias. In wildfire dispatch, that produces drone deployments over occupied housing for what was actually a controlled burn. This was the hardest call in the project.

A
Let TALON resolve the conflict silently

Sensor and satellite data update at different rates. A silent resolution can commit to the wrong conclusion before the conflict has even settled.

B
Block the screen with a modal until data catches up

Covering the map during a size-up removes the spatial context the operator needs most. A modal solves the wrong problem.

C
Surface the conflict, lock primary actions, require a verification checkbox

The Forced Calibrator. The operator confirms they checked the permit registry before anything re-enables. The map stays fully visible.

Trade-off

Adds 4–6 seconds of mandatory friction on every conflict. I accepted it because the friction is proportional to what it prevents — a deployment over occupied homes based on a burn the AI misread.

D—02

Showing the evidence behind the score, not just the score

AI transparency

TALON first surfaced a single number — "87% ignition probability." The operator could accept or override it, but had nothing concrete to interrogate. A score you can't examine turns the human into a rubber stamp.

A
Keep the aggregate score, add a collapsible "show reasoning" panel

Under time pressure, secondary panels don't get opened. The score becomes the only signal — same outcome as before, just with a panel that never gets used.

B
Surface only conflicting sources, hide agreeing ones

Hiding consensus removes calibration. If three sources agree and one conflicts, that ratio matters. Operators need to see how much of TALON's confidence is real before they can trust or override it.

C
Every source as its own chip — independent, legible, individually disputable

The evidence matrix. Each data source shows its own confirmation state. When TALON is uncertain, the operator can see exactly which signal is shaky — not just that confidence dropped.

Trade-off

More screen real estate, more visual complexity per decision. Worth it: operators who understand what drove a recommendation make meaningfully better override calls. The matrix is also what makes the Forced Calibrator legible — you need to see which source is in conflict, not just that the AI is uncertain.

D—03

Giving the AI a callsign, not a label

Accountability

The first build labelled everything generically — "AI confidence," "AI recommends," "AI action." A black box with a percentage attached. There was no way to tell, after the fact, whether a human decided something or the system did.

Every high-consequence system needs an attribution layer. So TALON got a callsign, a dedicated log colour (cyan against the operator's white), and a strict language register — system narration, not conversation. "Recalling Lidar-02 · taking over Scout-03." Not "I'm swapping your drone." The name is real. The character is deliberately absent.

The accountability this creates is structural, not cosmetic. After an incident, an investigator can reconstruct every step: what TALON initiated, what the operator confirmed, what was overridden and when. You can audit TALON-AUTO-RECALL-T+34s. You cannot audit "AI."

Trade-off

Naming creates an expectation. If TALON's messages drift toward conversational, operators start treating the system like a colleague — automation bias through the back door. The microcopy has to stay tight, impersonal, and format-consistent. That constraint is non-negotiable. The callsign is the accountability mechanism.

D—04

Deciding which agents surface to the operator and which run silently

AI boundary

Seven agents run during the Intelligence phase. Five complete silently. Two pause. The question wasn't which agents to gate — it was whether the mistake each agent could make was recoverable on its own.

Sensor Fusion, Fire Behavior, Resource Readiness, Operations Planner, and Governance all self-correct as new data arrives. If Fire Behavior models the spread direction wrong, the next telemetry pass updates it automatically. No gate needed.

Values at Risk calculates how many structures are inside the fire's projected path — the figure that determines escalation tier and which authorities get notified. If that number is wrong and doesn't surface, the wrong response level gets deployed. That error compounds in ways that can't be recovered mid-incident. So it surfaces.

Notification Brief doesn't require reasoning. Nothing exits the system as an official authority notification without explicit operator sign-off. Full stop.

Trade-off

Two gates add roughly 8–12 seconds to the 90-second window. That time is where the system's accountability lives — and running the pipeline under degraded sensor conditions confirmed those were the two points where silent resolution would have committed to the wrong answer.

D—05

Reducing interface density automatically as the incident escalates

Cognitive load

Early on, the same information density showed across every phase — including peak Operations, when the operator's attention is most divided. I considered a manual density toggle, but that adds a configuration decision at the worst possible moment.

Instead, density scales automatically: detailed cards in Intelligence collapse to single-line strips in Operations. Same layout grid throughout, so the operator's spatial model never resets — only the depth of information changes.

Trade-off

It takes a control away from the operator. That's the point — removing decisions during peak load is the feature, not a limitation of it.

Where AI couldn't help
Every one of these calls — where friction belongs, what stays human, how much to show someone who's already stressed — is a judgment AI couldn't make for me. Not because it lacks opinions, but because the answers depend on how operators actually think under pressure and what a system owes the people whose actions it amplifies. The judgment was the design work. AI handled the production so I had more time for the judgment.
05Edge cases & failure handling

Designed for the worst moment, not the demo.

A high-stakes system is defined by its worst moment, not its demo. Each state below names its trigger, what Asha sees, the recovery path, and where accountability is logged.

Designed backwards from failure
I used AI to generate plausible breakdown scenarios — bandwidth degradation curves, sensor disagreement timing, shift handover sequences under an active incident — then worked backward from each failure into the screen that had to absorb it. Building a working prototype meant I could trigger the Forced Calibrator, force the cell failback, and feel whether each authorization gate carried the right weight rather than guessing from static frames. 06How the design evolved

Three structural bets I committed to, then had to rethink.

The version that earned a runner-up finish isn't the version I started with. Each of these was a real decision I committed to — and each one had to change once I understood the problem better. The arc from first build to now is the design thinking.

V0.1 · discarded
One slider to escalate through every stage
What it was
A horizontal slider that moved through Assess → Contain → Rescue. Dragging it committed drones to preset positions for that stage. Fewer clicks, faster — it felt efficient.
Why it broke
It read like a volume knob. The operator was physically moving the interface forward, not making a decision. A gesture has no moment of commitment — and authorizing drones over homes is the one place you need one.
Now
Discrete confirmation gates. Every consequential transition takes an explicit action, not a drag position. This correction became the spine of the whole governance model — the Forced Calibrator, the authority sign-off, the shift-handover PIN all trace back to it.
V1 · corrected
Four equal phases across the whole response
What it was
Scan, Verify, Contain, and Rescue as four parallel stages with equal weight and identical density — the operator stepped through them like a wizard.
Why it broke
Scan and Verify are about assembling a decision; Contain and Rescue are about executing one. Treating them identically meant over-informing the operator during execution and under-informing them during size-up.
Now
Two cognitive modes — Intelligence and Operations. Density scales automatically between them on the same spatial grid. A dispatcher recognises the two modes instantly: size-up, then incident command. That legibility is what made the 90-second window credible.
Now · held
Redefining what the 90 seconds actually promises
What it was
The original prototype claimed the system could authorize full wildfire containment — staging drones, clearing evacuation routes, coordinating ground teams — all inside 90 seconds. A bold, cinematic number that made the demo look complete.
Why it broke
You cannot stage drones before a scout has verified the fire's location. You cannot evacuate residents before knowing which direction it's spreading. Any incident commander would end that conversation in under a minute. The claim was operationally indefensible.
The 90 seconds now end at authority notification. Sentinel's promise is compressed detection-to-verified-escalation — the first and most time-critical bottleneck in a real dispatch workflow. Containment and operations planning begin after, without an artificial clock. A less dramatic headline that survives contact with reality.

08Results & reflection

What it achieved — and what I still don't know.

In simulation walkthroughs against documented dispatch protocols, the verification flow ran in 88 seconds against a 48-minute manual baseline. These are the metrics I'd track to know whether it holds with real operators.

Primary validation
Time to first decision

Alert to dispatch action. Target under 15 seconds. Consistently above it means the brief isn't surfacing the right information first — a design problem, not a training one.

Safety signal
Dismissed-alert accuracy

What share of dismissed alerts turned out to be real incidents? Anything above 2% needs immediate review. The goal is accurate dismissals, not zero.

Trust calibration
Operator override rate

Target 15–40%. Too low means operators are rubber-stamping; too high means TALON's plans aren't reliable enough. Both are failure modes.

Governance check
Calibrator false-positive rate

How often does the Forced Calibrator fire on a real fire, not a controlled burn? Friction should be proportional to actual conflict probability.

What still needs real validation

The 90-second number comes from simulation, not trained operators under genuine pressure. Whether the Calibrator's friction is acceptable in a real dispatch crunch is something only field testing answers — and I've said so rather than dressing the estimate up as data.

What I'd change next time

Less time polishing the Operations density transitions, more time testing the evidence matrix with actual dispatchers. The open question: does the chip-based layout match how they reason about verification, or does their mental model work differently? I assumed it fits. I don't have the data to confirm it.

If the system can make a call without Asha knowing, Asha isn't in control — she just thinks she is.
